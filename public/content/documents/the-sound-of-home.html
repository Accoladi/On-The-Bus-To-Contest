Chapter One
Seven Pounds of Potato Salad

5:12 on Saturday Morning

At 5:12 on the third Saturday morning in September, I was standing behind Cumberland Hills High School with a six-foot plywood window balanced against my right shoulder and a small blue cooler dragging stubbornly behind me across the parking lot.

Years later, I would remember that morning differently.

I would remember the buses glowing beneath the yellow security lights and the mist lying low over the practice field. I would remember the first pale edge of sunrise over the trees and the sound of a trumpet warming up somewhere inside the building while most of Bowling Green was still asleep.

I would remember my mother’s lipstick.

Tom’s laugh.

A woman named Layla Rahman laughing beside me for the first time.

And Molly.

Most of all, I would remember Molly.

But at 5:12 that morning, I was mostly thinking about potato salad.

Seven pounds of it.

The potato salad was packed in three matching plastic bowls inside the cooler, surrounded by enough ice to prevent the Cumberland Hills Band Boosters from becoming the subject of a Warren County Health Department investigation.

The matching bowls had not been part of my original plan.

My original plan had been Tupperware.

Real Tupperware.

The kind manufactured during an era when kitchen storage apparently needed to survive both children and nuclear attack.

One bowl was faded yellow. Another was avocado green. The largest was orange, with a lid that required both thumbs and a personal relationship with Jesus to close.

The orange one had belonged to my grandmother.

WHITFIELD was still written across the bottom in black permanent marker, in the same slanted handwriting that had identified casserole dishes at church suppers for as long as I could remember.

I loved that bowl.

When I was little, potato salad appeared in it at Easter lunches, family reunions, funerals, and Sunday dinners when somebody stayed longer than expected. After Grandma died, the bowl eventually made its way through my mother’s kitchen and into mine, and for eleven years I used it without thinking very much about what that meant.

Then Molly became fourteen.

Fourteen changes the meaning of things.

At fourteen, an orange bowl from 1978 is not an heirloom. It is something your mother might carry into a room where your friends can see her.

Molly had never actually said she would be embarrassed by it.

She did not have to.

I remembered fourteen too well.

So, at 8:46 Friday night, while seven pounds of potatoes cooled in two colanders on the kitchen counter, I drove to Dollar General and bought the three largest matching plastic bowls they had.

Clear sides. Blue lids. Twelve dollars and change.

I had spent twelve dollars protecting my daughter’s dignity.

Motherhood, I was discovering, was filled with expenses nobody mentioned at the baby shower.

The cooler hit another crack in the asphalt and tilted hard to the right. I caught it with my leg before it toppled.

“Come on.”

The plywood window slid lower against my shoulder.

It was cream-colored with Cumberland Hills blue trim and a pair of white curtains stapled behind the opening. Someone had painted red flowers beneath the sill.

In the darkness they looked more like tomatoes.

Across the four-lane road, the yellow-and-black Waffle House sign glowed against the morning.

Then the smell found me.

Bacon.

Coffee.

Butter.

Hash browns crisping on the griddle.

It floated across the cool September air with an almost personal cruelty.

I stopped walking.

Through the windows I could see people sitting in booths beneath warm lights. They had coffee cups in their hands and plates in front of them. They were waiting for somebody else to bring them breakfast.

They were sitting down.

Normal people.

I had eaten half a banana over the kitchen sink at 4:41 while wearing one shoe.

There are moments in motherhood when another person’s plate of bacon can feel deeply personal.

The air was cool enough that I had pulled on a royal-blue Cumberland Hills quarter-zip before leaving home, but the familiar Kentucky humidity softened everything around the edges. Dew silvered the practice field. Moisture collected on the metal railings. The grass smelled wet and freshly cut, and beneath the diesel exhaust from the charter buses was the earthy smell of ground that had cooled overnight.

September in Bowling Green can lie to you before sunrise.

It whispers autumn.

By afternoon it remembers summer.

The forecast called for temperatures in the eighties.

I had dressed accordingly badly.

Jeans. White sneakers. The quarter-zip over a T-shirt I knew I would eventually need.

My hair had gone into a ponytail at 4:36 after I decided there was no relationship in my life important enough to justify a blow dryer at that hour.

I had managed mascara, concealer, and lip balm.

Then I had looked into the bathroom mirror and decided forty-four was simply going to have to be good enough.

The truth was, I didn’t dislike getting older as much as I disliked being surprised by it.

A gray strand near my right temple.

The fine lines beside my eyes.

The way I sometimes caught my reflection unexpectedly and saw my mother for half a second.

I used to think aging happened gradually.

It doesn’t.

It happens in glimpses.

So does childhood.

I didn’t completely understand that yet.

Not until Molly came through the band-room doors.

But that came later.

At 5:13, I was still hauling potato salad across the parking lot and trying not to lose part of a house.

Cumberland Hills High School was fully awake.

Two charter buses idled beside the band-room entrance, their engines sending a low rumble through the pavement. Exhaust drifted pale beneath the lights.

Parents arrived in a steady procession of SUVs and pickup trucks.

Doors slammed.

Coolers rolled over sidewalk seams.

Someone pushed a rack of uniforms toward the buses, the clear garment bags shifting slightly in the damp air.

A father in cargo shorts knelt beside a trailer hitch with a flashlight clenched between his teeth.

Three color-guard girls sat together on the curb in leggings and oversized sweatshirts. One held bobby pins between her lips while another pulled her hair into a bun so tight it looked capable of changing her personality.

A percussionist passed me carrying a snare harness in one hand and a Waffle House takeout box in the other.

I disliked him immediately.

From inside the building came three trumpet notes.

A pause.

The same three notes again.

A trombone answered from farther down the hallway, and then a tuba produced a low note that seemed almost too large for the hour.

A few seconds later the percussion room erupted in a burst of snare drumming.

I checked my watch.

5:13.

My daughter had joined a cult.

Chapter Two
At Your Own Risk

The decision had been announced the previous April.

Molly walked into the kitchen while I was loading the dishwasher and said she thought she wanted to do marching band.

I had been rinsing a plate.

“That sounds wonderful.”

There are sentences mothers say because our mouths occasionally operate several seconds ahead of our brains.

That was one of them.

What I should have said was:

Define marching band.

I had marched myself.

A long time ago.

Long enough ago that our uniforms had been made primarily from wool and punishment.

I knew about rehearsals, football games, black marching shoes, memorizing music, and band directors who believed one more time was a philosophical concept rather than a measurable quantity.

But marching band had apparently evolved since 1997.

There were props now.

Electronics.

Platforms.

Scenery.

Entire rooms.

Two weeks earlier, I had watched another band rehearse with something that appeared to be a twelve-foot tree.

In my marching-band days, scenery consisted of the fifty-yard line.

Nobody had ever asked my mother to transport a window before sunrise.

Then again, my mother probably would have refused.

She had occasionally been wiser than I gave her credit for.

Cumberland Hills’ new director of bands was Eli Mercer, a thirty-six-year-old Western Kentucky University graduate who had arrived that summer with the unenviable assignment of rebuilding a program people around Bowling Green still described with four words:

It used to be good.

I knew that phrase better than most.

There had been a time when Cumberland Hills’ band was large enough to fill the field and loud enough to make aluminum bleachers hum beneath your feet. There had been State Finals, championships, and years when people saw Cumberland Hills entering a stadium and paid attention.

I had been part of that once.

Briefly.

Mr. Mercer seemed to believe used to be was a dangerous thing to say around teenagers who were still deciding who they might become.

His first show was called HOME.

At the beginning, the band was divided by rolling walls into separate rooms. Windows moved. Doors opened and closed. A porch sat near one sideline. A kitchen table appeared somewhere in the middle.

By the end, the walls disappeared.

Everybody came together.

Home, apparently, wasn’t supposed to be a building.

It was supposed to be the people who remained when the walls were gone.

I told Molly I thought that sounded lovely.

She informed me it was not supposed to be lovely.

It was supposed to be emotional.

I apologized.

Apparently, there was a difference.

*****

“Lydia!”

I knew Darla Hensley’s voice before I found her.

She was moving toward me at a speed no fifty-three-year-old woman should possess before sunrise.

Darla was president of the Cumberland Hills Band Boosters, a position I had assumed was voluntary until I met her. After three weeks, I suspected she had been born into it.

She was short, compact, and alarmingly efficient, with auburn hair cut into a chin-length bob that somehow still looked neat before dawn.

I considered that suspicious.

She wore khaki shorts, white running shoes, a Cumberland Hills polo, and a fluorescent yellow safety vest.

There was no obvious danger requiring the vest.

Darla simply believed authority worked better when visible from low-flying aircraft.

Reading glasses rested on her face and another pair sat on top of her head. A radio hung from her belt. Three Sharpies protruded from her pocket.

If civilization collapsed before breakfast, Darla would have the survivors alphabetized and assigned to committees before lunch.

She looked relieved to see me.

I was less certain why.

“You have Window Two.”

I shifted the plywood against my shoulder.

“It has a number?”

“Everything has a number.”

That bothered me more than it should have.

She pointed toward Trailer One.

There were three trailers.

The explanation that followed involved a porch, two turns, the percussion hallway, and the trophy case. By the time I thought of a useful question, Darla was already walking away.

That was her particular gift.

She entered conversations like trains already in motion and left before anyone knew whether they were going in the same direction.

Across the back of her safety vest were the words:

CUMBERLAND HILLS BAND BOOSTERS

Below them:

ASK ME

Someone had added in black marker:

AT YOUR OWN RISK

Whoever had done that was someone I wanted to meet.

*****

Trailer One turned out to be the one with the porch.

Of course it did.

The porch was pale gray with white columns and enough decorative trim to suggest the show designer had recently discovered Pinterest.

Beside it stood four blue-framed windows, three rolling walls, a mailbox, two benches, and a wooden kitchen table.

An actual kitchen table.

I stared at it.

Then a man walked past carrying a fake front door.

I decided I was not emotionally prepared to ask questions.

Inside the school, the smell of the band hallway reached me before anything else did.

Brass polish.

Valve oil.

Coffee.

Reeds.

The faint sourness of teenage sweat trapped in a building that had seen thousands of rehearsals.

Smell is unfair that way.

It can return you somewhere before you have agreed to go.

For a second, I was sixteen again, carrying a clarinet case through a hallway I had not thought about in years.

Then one of the cooler wheels clicked against the floor, and the present returned.

I pulled the cooler down the hallway.

I tried not to look at the trophy cases.

I had become very good at not looking.

The glass reflected the overhead lights, but I knew what was behind it.

State plaques.

Championship photographs.

Band pictures from years when Cumberland Hills needed several rows just to fit everybody into the frame.

One photograph in particular had been there since 1997.

I knew exactly where I stood in it.

Second row.

Third from the end.

Clarinet in my lap.

Dark hair touching my shoulders.

Sixteen years old.

I had an expression on my face suggesting the future was something orderly that happened according to schedule.

I knew better now.

I kept walking.

Some memories are difficult because they were terrible.

Others are difficult because they were once happy.

The hospitality room rescued me.

Eight Crock-Pots lined one table. Grocery bags occupied another. Coolers were wedged beneath folding chairs, and a woman I did not know was labeling serving utensils with masking tape.

I opened my cooler.

The three Dollar General bowls looked perfectly respectable beside the coleslaw.

Almost elegant.

I felt an absurd amount of relief.

The woman handed me a Sharpie and asked for my name.

“Lydia Harper.”

She stared at me.

“On the bowls.”

“Oh.”

Band boosters, I was learning, had trust issues.

I wrote HARPER across three pieces of masking tape and pressed one onto each blue lid.

Then, because I had already lost control of the morning, I added my phone number.

Chapter Three
Stand Still

When I stepped outside again, the darkness had softened.

Not sunrise yet.

Just a blue-gray suggestion that morning was coming whether we were ready or not.

“Mom!”

I turned.

Molly was running toward me.

And something inside me went still.

There are days when your child changes so gradually you don’t notice.

Then there are mornings when the change seems to have happened while you were sleeping.

She was nearly my height now.

I knew that intellectually.

I had bought the jeans.

I had watched Tom mark another pencil line inside the laundry-room doorway when she grew nearly two inches over the summer.

But measurements don’t prepare you.

She was fourteen and slender in that almost-fragile way some girls are before their bodies decide what shape they intend to keep. Long legs. Narrow shoulders. Elbows she still had not quite learned where to put.

Her hair was dark chestnut brown, thick like mine had been at her age, with reddish-copper highlights that appeared whenever sunlight found them. That morning it had been pulled tightly into a braid that fell between her shoulder blades.

Her eyes were hazel.

Mine, though hers leaned greener.

Freckles crossed her nose, faint enough that she had recently begun covering them before school.

I had never told her I noticed.

There are things teenagers think they have hidden that mothers quietly agree not to reveal they have seen.

She wore black marching bibbers over a royal-blue shirt, and the uniform made her look older from a distance.

Up close, I could still see the child.

The same slight lift of one shoulder when she was uncertain.

The same freckles.

The same way her mouth pressed together before she asked for something.

For an instant, I saw the five-year-old who had insisted on carrying her own backpack into kindergarten and the fourteen-year-old crossing the parking lot toward me.

Both existed at once.

Then she reached me and accused me of forgetting her gloves.

The little girl disappeared.

Fourteen returned.

The gloves were exactly where I told her they were.

The dot book was another matter.

That was when I noticed her hand.

She was holding the clarinet case so tightly her knuckles had gone pale.

Her fingers were trembling.

Only a little.

Enough.

The irritation in her voice wasn’t really irritation.

I knew that because I had spent fourteen years learning the language beneath my daughter’s words.

She was afraid.

This was her first contest.

Her first time walking through a competition gate.

Her first time performing HOME for judges instead of teachers and parents.

For weeks she had complained about sunburn, sore feet, drill, music, dots, water breaks, and Mr. Mercer’s apparently limitless definition of again.

Now the day she had been preparing for had arrived.

There is something unfair about preparation.

Eventually the thing you have spent months preparing for expects you to do it.

“I don’t want to mess up.”

Her voice was quieter now.

For years, I had been the person Molly ran toward whenever she was afraid.

Scraped knees.

Thunderstorms.

First day of kindergarten.

Nightmares.

Lately fear had become private.

Bedroom doors closed.

Nothing replaced explanations.

Parts of her life had begun to belong only to her.

I knew that was normal.

Probably healthy.

I hated it anyway.

I reached toward the loose strand of hair beside her temple and smoothed it back.

She let me.

That was how nervous she was.

“You don’t have to be perfect.”

“Mr. Mercer might disagree.”

I almost smiled.

I knew enough about Eli Mercer by then to know she was wrong.

“What if I miss?” she asked.

I remembered being her age and believing a mistake could follow you forever.

At fourteen, every bad moment feels permanent because you haven’t lived long enough to learn how few things are.

“You do the thing after that.”

She studied me.

“That sounded like Grandma.”

That nearly ended the tenderness.

Before I could defend myself, a voice came from the band-room doorway.

“Harper.”

Nia Brooks stood there waiting.

Nia was Cumberland Hills’ assistant band director, thirty, tall and athletic, with black hair pulled into a smooth bun and posture so straight that other people seemed to correct theirs when she entered a room.

She had the remarkable ability to silence teenagers by raising one eyebrow.

Apparently, Molly’s dot book was sitting on the conductor’s podium where Molly herself had left it the night before.

Nia made certain I knew I was innocent.

I liked Nia Brooks.

Molly turned toward the building.

“Molly.”

She looked back.

The sky had brightened enough that the first sunlight caught the copper in her hair.

I wanted to say something useful.

Something memorable.

Something she might carry with her when she stepped onto the field that evening.

What came out was:

“Have a wonderful day.”

Mothers are often least articulate when the moment matters most.

She smiled anyway.

“I’ll try.”

Then she went inside.

I kept watching the doorway after she disappeared.

For fourteen years, nearly every important moment in Molly’s life had required me to move closer.

Pick her up.

Hold her hand.

Tie the shoe.

Walk her into the classroom.

Sit beside the bed.

This one required the opposite.

I had to stay where I was and let her go.

Maybe that is one of the harder parts of raising children.

Eventually love stops asking you to hold on.

Sometimes it asks whether you can stand still while they walk away.

Chapter Four
Miss June

“She’s getting tall.”

I knew the voice before I turned.

My mother, June Whitfield, stood behind me with a stadium cushion tucked beneath one arm and a rhinestone-covered travel mug in the other.

Almost everybody in Bowling Green called her Miss June.

I never had.

To me she was Mother when I was being polite, Mama when I was tired or sentimental, and occasionally several other things I kept to myself.

Molly had called her Grandma June when she was little. At fourteen it had shortened to

Grandma unless she wanted something.

Mother was seventy-one and had owned a bridal shop in Bowling Green for more than thirty years, which meant she could connect almost anyone in Warren County to a wedding, bridesmaid, mother of the bride, minister, florist, caterer, organist, or cousin.

Retirement had not reduced her social reach.

It had simply given her more time to use it.

She also looked better at 5:31 in the morning than I did at noon.

Her silver-blonde hair was styled in a soft layered bob. She wore dark jeans, white leather sneakers, a Cumberland Hills fleece vest, small gold hoop earrings—

and lipstick.

Actual lipstick.

Before sunrise.

My mother had standards.

When I was a child, I spent countless afternoons in the back room of Whitfield Bridal doing homework while she pinned hems, adjusted veils, and calmed women who had suddenly become convinced their wedding dresses were wrong.

Mother had a way of standing a nervous bride in front of a mirror and placing both hands lightly on her shoulders.

“Shoulders back, sweetheart. You’re allowed to take up space.”

She said it to brides.

She said it to me.

I never became as comfortable being looked at as she was.

Mother entered a room prepared to be seen.

I became very good at entering unnoticed.

She looked toward the door where Molly had disappeared.

“She looks like you did.”

“No, she doesn’t.”

My answer came too quickly.

Mother noticed.

She always did.

“She does around the eyes.”

“She has Tom’s eyes when she’s mad.”

“No. Those are yours too.”

There was a time when a sentence like that would have meant nothing.

Standing ten yards from the same band room I had walked away from almost thirty years earlier, it meant too much.

Mother took a sip from her rhinestone cup.

“You’re back here.”

“Molly is back here.”

“I know what I said.”

“And I know what you meant.”

Her lips pressed together.

Not anger.

Mother rarely became openly angry.

She became mannerly.

There was a difference.

“I was only making an observation.”

“You’ve never made only an observation in your life.”

“Lydia.”

The way she said my name was pleasant enough for church.

That was how I knew I had reached something tender.

For a moment neither of us said anything.

I could hear the buses idling.

Someone laughing near the trailers.

A snare drum tapping inside the building.

Mother stared past me toward the band-room doors.

Something in her expression changed, though I could not have said exactly what.

Regret, perhaps.

Or memory.

Before either of us could continue, I saw Tom walking toward us.

He slowed when he noticed our faces.

My husband had spent more than twenty years learning the weather patterns between my mother and me.

He could identify a storm from fifty yards away.

He looked first at Mother.

Then at me.

“Morning, June.”

“Good morning, Tom.”

Perfectly pleasant.

He kissed my cheek.

Then he looked at me long enough to ask a question without saying it.

You two all right?

I gave him the answer married people learn to give the same way.

Not now.

Tom exhaled almost invisibly.

Weariness crossed his face for only a moment.

Not anger.

Not even annoyance.

Just the exhaustion of a man who loved two women who could injure one another without ever raising their voices.

Then he smiled at Mother.

“Nice vest.”

Her face brightened.

“Thank you.”

And the storm moved offshore.

For the moment.

Chapter Five
Harper Family Incorporated

Tom had not planned to come that early.

Darla had apparently obtained his telephone number through a welder named Ray and asked him to look at something loose beneath the porch.

When Tom said look, I laughed.

By then I understood enough about band boosters to know nobody ever simply looked at anything.

At forty-seven, Tom was heavier than the man I had married, but somehow more solid rather than simply older. Years of hauling lumber, climbing ladders, and refusing to ask younger employees to lift anything he wouldn’t lift himself had kept his shoulders broad and his forearms strong.

Silver had begun showing at his temples.

I hated him for that.

Gray hair on me looked like something requiring corrective action.

On Tom it looked intentional.

For a moment I watched him walk toward the trailer and remembered a summer twenty years earlier when he built the deck behind our first house.

He had worked without a shirt because the heat was unbearable.

I sat on the back steps with a paperback novel I never read.

I remembered watching the muscles move across his shoulders as he lifted one board after another.

He caught me staring once.

I pretended to return to the same page I had been pretending to read for ten minutes.

He smiled.

I had not thought about that afternoon in years.

That bothered me.

There had once been a time when I noticed everything about my husband.

The way he leaned against a doorway.

The smell of sawdust on his shirts.

The small scar near his left wrist.

The way his voice changed when he was tired.

Now there were days when we moved through the same house exchanging schedules rather than thoughts.

Nothing was wrong.

That was what I told myself.

There had been no betrayal.

No great fight.

No slammed doors.

Our marriage had simply become efficient.

Tom ran Harper Construction.

I worked as a paralegal for a small firm downtown.

We paid bills.

Shared calendars.

Bought groceries.

Got Molly where she needed to go.

We knew whose turn it was to buy dog food and which week the air-conditioning filters needed changing.

We had become excellent managers of Harper Family Incorporated.

Somewhere along the way, I was no longer certain we were equally good at being Tom and Lydia.

There was no villain in our marriage.

That almost made it harder.

Tom glanced back toward me before disappearing beside the trailer.

For a second, I thought he might come back.

He didn’t.

Neither did I.

There are distances in marriage that cannot be measured in feet.

Chapter Six
What It Is Now

A dark gray SUV entered the parking lot several minutes later.

Layla Rahman stepped from the passenger side.

I knew her from booster meetings, although knew was generous.

We had exchanged names.

Sat beside each other once.

Talked long enough for me to understand that she had moved to Bowling Green less than two months earlier and was still measuring everything here against what she had left behind.

She was forty-three, with warm brown skin, dark eyes, and a round face that became almost luminous when she smiled.

That morning she wasn’t smiling.

She wore jeans, comfortable black shoes, a long camel-colored cardigan, and a slate-blue hijab wrapped neatly around her head.

Her husband, Amir, came around the SUV carrying a saxophone backpack their son had forgotten.

Amir was tall, with a close-cropped black beard beginning to gray along the jaw, rectangular glasses, khaki pants, and a Detroit Lions quarter-zip.

The shirt said, without needing words, that part of him still belonged somewhere else.

The Rahmans had moved from the Dearborn area in July after Amir accepted an engineering position at Bowling Green Assembly.

The Corvette plant.

In Bowling Green, that was usually enough explanation.

People understood good jobs at the plant.

What they did not always understand was what a good opportunity could cost the people who came with you.

At a booster meeting, Layla had told me Amir had wanted this position for years.

She said it proudly.

Then she looked down at her hands.

I had remembered that.

Sam appeared in the band-room doorway.

Freshman.

Alto saxophone.

Fourteen.

Thin, with thick black hair falling toward his eyes and the wary expression of a boy who had not agreed to become the new kid.

Amir held up the backpack.

Sam came down the steps.

For a moment father and son stood facing each other.

Same dark eyes.

Same stubborn mouth.

Amir handed him the bag and wished him good luck.

Sam said it wasn’t luck.

It was exactly the sort of answer fourteen-year-olds give—technically correct and sharpened just enough to remind a parent that whatever anger existed had not disappeared overnight.

Amir accepted it.

Before Sam went back inside, Layla called his name.

He turned.

She placed one hand lightly over her heart.

Nothing more.

Sam saw her.

His face softened.

Only slightly.

But I noticed.

Mothers always notice.

We learn our children’s faces the way people learn maps of places they love.

The small changes.

The roads nobody else sees.

The places where worry settles.

Layla watched Sam disappear through the door.

I found myself wondering how many doors in Michigan she had watched him walk through without fear.

How many people there already knew his name.

How many grandparents, cousins, neighbors, teachers, mosque friends, and classmates had surrounded him without anybody having to try.

Here, every relationship had to begin again.

I knew almost nothing about Layla’s life.

But I knew enough about leaving things behind to recognize someone still learning what could be carried and what could not.

Then Amir noticed the porch.

That was how it began.

Tom was underneath one of the platforms.

Only his boots were visible.

Amir stopped.

He looked.

Tilted his head.

Layla saw it too.

“No.”

I looked at her.

She nodded toward her husband.

“He has seen something.”

There was resignation in her voice.

Affection too.

Within a minute Amir was crouched beside the platform, pointing toward a caster. Another minute passed and both men were underneath it.

Layla closed her eyes.

I laughed.

Then she laughed.

At first quietly.

Then hard enough that she covered her mouth.

It was the first time I had heard her laugh without restraint.

For that moment she did not look like a woman who had moved six hundred miles and was trying to learn a new town.

She simply looked happy.

I watched her, and something inside me relaxed.

We were still almost strangers.

But not quite as much as we had been five minutes earlier.

*****

At 5:55, Eli Mercer came through the band-room doors.

I had seen him almost every day since band camp began, usually from a distance, standing above the practice field.

Up close, he looked younger.

Thirty-six.

A little over six feet.

Lean, with long arms and the straight-backed posture of somebody accustomed to conducting. His dark hair was cut short, with the first traces of gray above his ears.

But it wasn’t his appearance that changed the parking lot.

‘It was everybody else.

A sophomore stopped joking.

Two clarinet players straightened.

A percussionist closed his breakfast box.

Molly stopped touching the keys on her clarinet.

Sixty-seven teenagers became more aware of themselves simply because Eli Mercer had stepped outside.

That told me more about him than a biography could have.

He checked the buses.

The trailers.

The sky.

Then the band.

He seemed to notice several things at once without looking hurried.

Maybe that was part of being a good band director.

Or a good parent.

Or simply a competent adult.

I was still working on all three.

Mercer called the band together.

They gathered beneath a sky beginning to turn pale gold.

Sixty-seven young people.

The number looked small.

I hated myself for noticing.

The Cumberland Hills band I remembered had filled space differently. There had been enough of us that the field itself seemed to shrink when everyone arrived.

But those students were gone.

I was one of them.

And here stood Molly.

Sam.

Sixty-five others.

Mercer looked across the group.

“This is our first Saturday.”

His voice was calm.

A breeze moved through the trees behind the practice field.

Then he said, “Don’t spend today proving Cumberland Hills used to be good.”

Something inside me tightened.

Behind me, I could feel the school building even without turning.

The trophy case.

The 1997 photograph.

The girl who believed she would finish everything she started.

Mercer let the silence sit for a moment.

“Show them what it is now.”

That was all.

No screaming.

No promise that they were about to shock Kentucky.

No speech about destiny.

He didn’t tell them they were special.

He asked them to show it.

A senior lowered his head.

A freshman trumpet player stood straighter.

Sam looked up.

Molly became perfectly still.

For nearly thirty years, I had treated Cumberland Hills marching band as something behind me.

A photograph.

An unfinished season.

A place where I used to belong.

Now my daughter was standing in its uniform.

For the first time that morning, I understood that used to be did not belong only to the band.

It belonged to me too.

Chapter Seven
We Need Ice

The formation broke apart.

Noise returned immediately.

Cases were loaded.

Parents hugged children who pretended not to want to be hugged.

Darla resumed directing civilization.

Chapter Sixteen
The New Boy from Detroit

Everybody at Cumberland Hills said Sam Rahman was from Detroit.

For the first week of band camp, Sam corrected them.

“Dearborn.”

He never sounded offended when he said it. He simply supplied the proper answer and went on with whatever he had been doing, as if correcting geography were no different from correcting a wrong note or telling somebody they had called an alto saxophone a tenor.

Detroit was close enough for everyone else. It was not close enough for Sam.

By the following Monday, he had stopped correcting people.

I noticed because I had begun noticing Sam.

Not deliberately. I wasn't studying him because he was new, or Muslim, or from Michigan, or because adults have a habit of turning children into projects the minute we think they might need help adjusting. Late July in Bowling Green was not interested in anyone's comfort.

Cicadas screamed from the trees beyond the end zone with the relentless confidence of creatures that had apparently been promised one season and intended to use all of it.

People who romanticize Southern summers have generally experienced them from a porch with a ceiling fan and something cold in their hands.

Band camp was different.

The air held the smell of valve oil, damp T-shirts, brass, sunblock and the faint exhaust from delivery trucks making their way behind the cafeteria. Chapter Twenty-Eight
The Sign Above the Doors

Same expressions.

WHEN YOU SAY YOUR BLESSING,

Layla watched without moving.

Across the room, Darla stood near the end of the serving line with a stack of napkins in her hands. She separated them, tapped their edges against the table, and placed them beside the utensils.

The napkins were already straight.

Her eyes followed Sam until he sat with Marcus and Tyler.

Then she adjusted the stack one final time.

No one congratulated her.

No one needed to.

Beside me, Layla released a breath so quietly that I felt it more than heard it.

Her son had received a sandwich.

That was all.

It should always have been all.

But the difference between being handed something and being considered before you arrive can be larger than the thing itself.

Across the cafeteria, Tyler Watkins placed a plastic spoon beneath his nose and attempted to hold it against his upper lip.

Marcus Bell watched for several seconds before knocking it onto the table.

Tyler objected.

Sam said something I could not hear.

Whatever it was made Marcus laugh and Tyler throw the spoon at Sam’s chest.

Sam caught it before it fell into his food.

For a few moments, the three boys argued about something that had nothing to do with Michigan, Kentucky, religion, dietary requirements, or the challenge of being new.

They were simply fourteen-year-old boys behaving badly with cafeteria utensils.

Ordinary.

The freedom to be ordinary.

That was what Layla had wanted for him.

Not constant accommodation.

Not special attention.

Not the burden of representing everyone who shared his faith or background.

Only the chance to enter a lunch line, sit with friends, make a joke, and have someone throw a spoon at him without the moment acquiring greater meaning.

I looked at Layla.

She was smiling toward the boys.

She had seen it too.

“Thank you,” she said.

“We’re learning.”

Her gaze remained on Sam.

“So is he.”

Molly reached their table carrying a sandwich, a bag of chips, and enough sliced fruit to suggest someone in hospitality still believed teenagers could be persuaded toward responsible choices.

Two clarinet players followed her. The three girls took the empty seats across from Sam, Marcus, and Tyler, closing the space between the two groups until six lunch trays nearly covered the table.

Molly had been seated less than a minute when she picked up a french fry and began using it as a conductor’s baton.

From where I stood, I could not hear every word. I caught something about the wall near Side Two, Sam’s entrance, and whether the saxophones were moving too early or the clarinets were moving too late.

Sam shook his head.

Molly drew an invisible line above the table with the french fry.

“That’s not where the form turns.”

“It is from my side.”

“Your side is wrong.”

“A side cannot be wrong.”

“Yours found a way.”

Marcus lowered his head over his tray, already smiling.

Tyler looked from Molly to Sam with the quiet pleasure of someone who had discovered entertainment requiring no effort from him.

Sam leaned forward and traced his own version of the drill against the tabletop with one finger. Molly interrupted before he reached the end.

He raised one hand, asking for enough silence to complete his defense.

She raised both of hers, apparently presenting the entire field as evidence.

The two clarinet players exchanged a look suggesting they had seen this before and expected to see it many times again.

I could not determine which of them was correct.

I was not entirely certain either one cared anymore.

The argument had become less about the drill than the pleasure of finding someone who would continue answering.

Whatever they were discussing, neither intended to surrender.

And neither seemed to want the other one to.

Layla watched them for another moment.

“She likes to argue.”

“That is one interpretation.”

Layla turned toward me.

“What is yours?”

“She believes every conversation should have a winner.”

Layla looked back at Sam.

“So does he.”

Molly’s french fry made another decisive movement through the air.

Sam folded his arms and leaned back with the expression of a boy preparing a rebuttal.

“They are well matched,” Layla said.

Something in her voice made me look at her.

She was teasing me.

Probably.

I returned my attention to the table.

Molly was fourteen.

Sam was fourteen.

They were discussing marching drill, music, or whether a soprano saxophone looked like a clarinet that had made bad choices.

There was no reason for the phrase well matched to trouble me.

It did anyway.

Motherhood had turned me into a woman capable of imagining three years beyond any conversation involving my daughter.

I decided not to examine that too closely.

Chapter Thirty-Two
Use Words

At twelve-twenty, I checked my watch.

The minute hand had crossed into territory that would require either an explanation at the law firm or a different definition of employment.

Outside the cafeteria windows, the July sun lay white against the pavement. My car had been sitting beneath it for almost two hours. The steering wheel would be too hot to touch, and the inside would smell faintly of the fast-food bag Molly left beneath the passenger seat three days earlier.

I thought of my desk downtown.

The files stacked beside my computer.

The telephone messages waiting beneath a paperweight.

The quiet, climate-controlled order of the law office.

That life already seemed farther away than the seven-minute drive between it and Cumberland Hills.

I had arrived intending to place labels on tables.

Instead, I had learned the name of Darla’s dead husband, watched a mother see that her son had been remembered, and spent part of my lunch break measuring the emotional significance of a sandwich.

Marching band was becoming unreasonable that way.

It kept reaching into places that appeared to have nothing to do with music.

I collected my purse from beneath the hospitality table and slipped its strap over my shoulder.

Darla met me near the cafeteria doors.

She had positioned herself slightly to one side, leaving enough room for students to pass, though the clipboard tucked beneath her arm suggested I was not leaving until one final matter had been settled.

With her free hand, she removed her reading glasses and let them hang from the collar of her shirt. A faint red mark remained where they had rested against the bridge of her nose.

“Tomorrow we need—”

She stopped.

Her lips remained parted around the unfinished sentence.

Then her eyes lifted to mine.

For half a second, I could see the old words waiting.

Tomorrow we need ice.

An assignment disguised as information.

Darla shifted the clipboard beneath her arm. Her fingers tightened along its edge, and something almost resembling embarrassment crossed her face.

She had remembered.

Not gracefully.

Not soon enough to prevent the sentence from beginning.

But she had remembered.

She drew a small breath and tried again.

“Would you be able to bring ice tomorrow?”

The question sounded recently assembled, as though she had constructed it from unfamiliar parts and was not yet certain it would hold together.

But it was a question.

I let her wait just long enough for both of us to recognize that.

Then I adjusted the strap of my purse.

“How much?”

The tension left her shoulders so quickly that another person might not have noticed it had been there.

“Sixteen bags.”

“What size?”

“Ten-pound bags.”

“What time?”

“Ten-thirty.”

She had returned to familiar territory now.

Numbers.

Quantities.

Schedules.

By the time she gave me the time, her voice had regained most of its usual authority. But the authority felt different because she had asked permission before using it.

I nodded.

“Text it to me.”

“I will.”

“With words.”

A narrow line appeared between Darla’s eyebrows.

“With the photograph,” I explained. “Please send words.”

“I always send words.”

“Yesterday you sent me a picture of a cooler.”

“It was empty.”

“That described the cooler. It did not describe your expectations.”

Darla lowered her chin and studied me across the top edge of the clipboard.

“You knew it needed ice.”

“I knew it had no ice. For all I knew, you were sharing a photograph of a cooler that had achieved its purpose.”

Her mouth tightened.

At first, I thought I had irritated her.

Then one corner betrayed her.

The movement was so slight that it might have escaped someone who had not spent the morning learning how much Darla concealed behind clipboards and instructions.

She was trying not to smile.

“I’ll include words,” she said.

“Thank you.”

“And the photograph.”

“Fine.”

She pulled the clipboard from beneath her arm and wrote something near the bottom of the page.

Earlier that morning, I would have assumed she was adding another task to my name.

This time I asked.

“What did you write?”

Darla angled the clipboard toward her body, shielding the page from me.

“Use words.”

For an instant, she kept her expression perfectly composed.

Then the corner of her mouth moved again.

I laughed before I could stop myself.

The sound surprised both of us.

Darla slipped her reading glasses back onto her face and pushed them into place with one finger. She looked pleased, though she attempted to hide it by inspecting whatever she had written.

She was not especially good at humor.

She was working on that too.

I pushed open the cafeteria door.

The cooler air of the hallway met me first. Behind me, students continued talking across tables. Someone dropped a plastic tray. A cafeteria worker called for more napkins. Tyler’s voice rose above the others in protest about something he almost certainly deserved.

I looked back.

Darla had already returned to the serving line.

Layla stood near the table watching Sam.

Molly pointed the remains of her french fry toward him while he objected with his entire face.

Above them, the football players remained frozen beneath their old prayer for safety and victory.

That morning, nothing dramatic had happened in the Cumberland Hills cafeteria.

A few tables had been moved.

Labels had been displayed.

A boy received a sandwich.

One woman asked instead of assigning.

Another agreed to return.

But life rarely announces which small moments are changing us.

Most of the time, it lets them pass disguised as ordinary things.

A photograph.

A question.

A place at a table.

A meal someone remembered before you arrived.

I stepped into the hallway and allowed the cafeteria door to close behind me.

Then I hurried toward the parking lot.

I was already going to need an explanation.

Chapter Thirty-Three
Water Beneath a Door

By Friday, hospitality had spread through my workweek like water beneath a door. There had been no single moment when I could have pointed to the damage and said, There. That is when it happened. A telephone call became three. A question about sandwich bread required a conversation with a supplier. Somewhere between Tuesday’s lunch and Thursday’s missing delivery, I began keeping a second notepad beside my keyboard, and by Friday the band’s notepad had more writing in it than the law firm’s.

I answered food vendors in the careful, pleasant voice I usually reserved for clients who had misplaced something we had asked them not to misplace. Ingredient lists lay beneath legal filings. While the printer warmed up, I calculated how many people a catering tray actually fed, as opposed to how many people the catering company believed it ought to feed.

The difference appeared to be percussion.

At eleven each morning, I exchanged the law office’s cool, carpeted quiet for the heat rising off the school parking lot. Sunlight flashed against windshields, and the short walk to the cafeteria was enough to make my blouse cling beneath my arms. Before I reached the serving tables, somebody generally needed an answer.

Where were the extra gloves? Had the fruit arrived? Did I know whether the adults working on props had been included in the count?

I ate standing up, when I remembered to eat at all.

By twelve-thirty, or one, or whenever the latest problem had become manageable enough to leave with someone else, the backs of my heels were tender from walking in shoes intended for an office. I drove downtown with the air-conditioning pointed at my face and rehearsed an explanation for being late.

Back at my desk, I could still smell the cafeteria on myself: mustard, cleaner, the warm, sour edge of rehearsal clothes. Once, while reaching across a conference table to hand a client a document, I discovered a disposable glove in my jacket pocket.

It was unused. That seemed worth being grateful for.

My supervisor had been patient. He accepted my hurried explanations with a nod and quietly moved one meeting to accommodate me. But on Friday afternoon, he appeared beside my desk holding a file against his ribs and remained there after I finished typing.

“Everything all right at home?”

I lifted my hands from the keyboard. “Yes.”

“At the school?”

“Yes.”

His eyes moved over the papers beside my computer. A serving chart lay on top of a property description.

“With the band?”

I leaned back in my chair. “How do you know it’s the band?”

“You have blue tape on your shoe.”

I looked down. A strip of masking tape clung to the heel of my black pump, its loose end folded around a small gray collection of parking-lot dirt. I had walked through reception wearing it. Possibly the conference room as well.

“It’s band camp,” I said, bending to peel it off.

“I gathered.”

He waited while I rolled the tape into a ball. His expression was not unkind, which made me feel worse than a reprimand would have. A reprimand would at least have given me something to resent.

“You know you have vacation time.”

“I’m saving it.”

“For what?”

“A trip with my mother. In October.”

He nodded, then placed the file beside my keyboard with the tab facing me. Before he could speak again, my phone vibrated against the desk. We both looked at it.

Three messages from Darla.

“I think,” he said gently, “you may need to decide which place you’re going to be next week.”

After he returned to his office, I turned my phone facedown. I squared the file with the edge of my desk and opened it, determined to finish something that belonged to the people who paid me.

Chapter Thirty-Four
Next Year Has Signed Nothing

At four-fifteen, Darla called.

I nearly let it ring.

When I answered, she did not begin with a quantity or a question about coolers. She said my name and paused, and I sat a little straighter.

The parent scheduled to coordinate meals for the final week of camp was in an emergency room in Louisville. Her mother had fallen and fractured her hip. There would be surgery, then decisions about care, and she had no idea when she could come home.

“Oh, Darla.”

“I told her not to think about any of this.”

“Of course.”

Through the glass in my supervisor’s office door, I could see him talking on the telephone, one hand pressed against his forehead. Down the hall, a drawer slid shut. Somewhere near reception, someone laughed.

“Is she up there alone?” I asked.

“Her sister’s driving in.”

“That’s good.”

“Yes.”

I shifted the phone to my other ear. I knew what was coming and wished that knowing it did not make me want to end the call.

“Lydia, would you be willing to take next week?”

She had asked. I heard the care she took with the sentence.

“I’m supposed to be here, Darla. At work.”

“I know.”

“The whole week?”

“I can find people to serve. But I need someone who knows what we’ve put together.”

I looked at the second notepad. There were supplier numbers in my handwriting, a crossed-out bread order, a reminder to keep the sealed sandwiches separate. Until that moment, I had thought of those pages as evidence that I was helping. Now they looked like qualifications.

I felt terrible for the woman in Louisville. I imagined her beneath the emergency-room lights with her mother’s purse in her lap, waiting for someone to come through a door.

I still resented being needed in her place.

“Let me see what I can do,” I said.

Darla let out a breath. “Thank you.”

“It isn’t a yes yet.”

“I understand.”

She did understand. She also began explaining Monday.

Within fifteen minutes, I had turned to a clean page and filled most of it. There were sixty-seven students, then staff, Western students, parent volunteers, prop builders, and occasional alumni whose affection for Cumberland Hills seemed to strengthen around lunchtime. Orders needed confirming. Volunteers needed finding. Someone had to check food temperatures and set out the correct utensils, clean tables, label leftovers, and discover why Wednesday’s percussion schedule required twelve additional lunches.

As Darla talked, I rubbed a pale ring on my desk with my thumb. Someone had left a coffee cup there years before I came to work at the firm. Usually I did not notice it. That afternoon I traced its entire circumference while Darla read me the delivery schedule.

“I’ll call you back,” I told her.

At four-thirty, I went into the restroom and locked the door.

The room smelled of lavender hand soap and the old building’s pipes. A folded paper towel had been tucked beneath the soap dispenser to catch a leak. I rested my hands on the sink and looked at myself in the mirror.

A strand of hair had escaped near my temple. There was a mustard stain on my cuff, dried darker at the edges where I had tried to remove it with water. I turned my wrist inward.

My supervisor’s office was only a few steps down the hall. I called him anyway. I could not quite bear to stand in his doorway and watch him be understanding.

“I need to take next week as vacation,” I said.

He was quiet briefly. “Has something happened?”

I explained the fall, the woman in Louisville, the meals. Speaking it aloud in that little room made the request sound both reasonable and faintly absurd.

“All right,” he said. “Send me a list of what needs covering before you leave.”

I thanked him, more than once.

“And Lydia? Leave the work here.”

After we hung up, I stayed at the sink. My phone lit again.

Darla had sent: NEED ICE PLAN.

Three words. She was keeping her promise about using words.

I pressed the heel of my hand against my forehead and laughed once, without much pleasure.

The week I had just taken was the last full week of vacation I had available that year. Mother and I had been saving it for October.

We were going to Greenville, South Carolina, to visit relatives she had not seen since before the pandemic. She had already divided them into people who required a visit, people who required a meal, and people who could be managed over coffee. One cousin’s house was to be avoided after dark because the driveway, according to Mother, was “an invitation to litigation.”

On the way home, we planned to spend two nights in Gatlinburg.

She wanted a balcony facing the mountains. When she first mentioned it, I reminded her that we would hardly be in the room.

“Well, I intend to be,” she said. “I’m not driving all that way to look at a parking lot.”

Afterward, I began imagining that balcony, too. Two chairs, a small table, coffee cooling between us. Red and gold along the ridges and the sort of morning air that made you pull your sleeves over your hands. Mother would bring a new sweater and explain, before I asked, how little she had paid for it.

We might spend an hour without needing to get anywhere.

A few days earlier, she had called to ask whether I still liked the little peanut-butter crackers.

“What crackers?”

“The ones you used to eat in the car.”

“I haven’t thought about those in years.”

“Well, think about them. I’m making a list.”

When I was little, she kept a package in her purse on long trips. She would open it along the seam and fold the wrapper back so I could take one without scattering crumbs, then hold it over her shoulder while she watched the road ahead. Sometimes I fell asleep before I finished, and woke with her cardigan tucked between my cheek and the hot window.

At forty-four, I could buy my own crackers. I had told her to put them on the list.

I called her Friday evening from the kitchen.

Tom was outside repairing a section of fence. Through the window, I watched him brace a board against his thigh, examining its edge before he put it in place. Molly’s rehearsal clothes tumbled in the dryer, a zipper tapping the drum with every turn. My work bag sat on a kitchen chair, still zipped.

Mother answered brightly.

“I was just looking at our room.”

I closed my eyes.

“What room?”

“In Gatlinburg. There’s one on a higher floor for almost the same price. I thought we ought to—”

“Mother.”

She stopped.

I gripped the edge of the counter and explained.

When I finished, I could hear paper moving on her end. Then that stopped, too.

“Of course,” she said.

Her voice had lost its brightness.

“I’m sorry.”

“I know you wouldn’t do it for nothing.”

“There’s a woman whose mother broke her hip. She’s in Louisville, and there isn’t—”

“You told me, sweetheart.”

I looked down at the counter. A crescent of dried milk marked the place where Molly had left her breakfast bowl. I rubbed it with my thumbnail.

“I do understand,” Mother said. “I’m allowed to be disappointed.”

“I didn’t say you weren’t.”

“No. You’re explaining it again.”

I stopped rubbing.

“I wanted to go,” I said.

“So did I.”

Outside, Tom set his hammer on the patio table. He bent to pick up another board, disappeared below the window, then rose again.

I waited for Mother to say something else.

Finally, she asked, “Is there truly nobody else?”

“Not anyone who can step in this late and knows the system.”

“Could several people do it?”

“Someone would still have to coordinate them.”

“And that someone has to be you.”

There was no accusation in her voice. I almost wished there had been.

“I told them I would.”

She took a breath. I could picture her at her dining-room table with the trip folder open, her reading glasses low on her nose. Hotel information on one side, relatives’ telephone numbers on the other. She still wrote directions beneath addresses, as though the phone might fail and leave us stranded among our own kin.

“We can go another time,” I said.

“The leaves will not consult our schedule.”

“Then next year.”

She was quiet before she answered.

“People say that as though next year has signed a contract.”

I lifted my head.

Mother was seventy-one. Most days, that meant very little to either of us. She still bought shoes with the confidence of a woman expecting plenty of occasions to wear them. But lately, when a name came up, she might tell me someone had stopped driving, or moved in with a daughter, or no longer remembered her when she called.

“Is somebody in Greenville not doing well?”

“Oh, everybody has something.”

“Mother.”

“No particular crisis, Lydia.”

I heard her clear her throat.

“I just thought we could—” She stopped and began again, more quietly. “I wanted us to go.”

I pulled out a kitchen chair and sat down.

“I know.”

The words came out barely above a whisper. I swallowed and pressed my free hand flat against the table.

For once, I could not think of anything useful to offer.

“We’ll talk about it later,” she said.

She asked whether Molly was drinking enough water. I said she was. She told me to make sure Tom ate something besides whatever he could carry into the yard. We exchanged the small instructions by which we usually took care of one another, and then she said she needed to let me go.

After we hung up, I remained at the table with the phone in my hand.

I wanted to call her back. I wanted her to answer in the bright voice she had used before I told her, and describe the higher room, and let me say that a balcony sounded lovely.

I could almost feel the cold morning air against my face. Mother would complain that the coffee was weak, drink it anyway, and ask whether I wanted to sit a little longer before we got dressed.

I wanted that morning.

What I did not want was the moment that might come after it, when she set down her cup and said there was something she had been meaning to ask me.

We had learned to keep certain conversations brief. There was always dinner to finish, an appointment to get to, Molly needing one of us. On a trip, there would be long stretches of highway and evenings with no one else in the room. Sooner or later, Cumberland Hills would come up. The year I left the band. What Mother remembered, and what I had never managed to tell her.

Now there would be no reason to brace myself when she grew quiet beside me.

The relief was small but unmistakable. I sat with it, ashamed.

I knew how to solve next week’s problems. Even the complicated ones could be written down. I could count lunches, telephone a supplier, ask someone to arrive half an hour earlier. Before this evening was over, I would send Darla an ice plan, and she would thank me, and something would be settled.

Mother had asked for a few days with me.

Outside, Tom pushed against the repaired fence board to test it. The dryer stopped, leaving the kitchen suddenly quiet.

I opened Mother’s last message about the trip.

She had sent a picture of the mountains from the hotel balcony. Beneath it she had written, Wouldn’t this be nice?

I sat with it on the screen until the light went out.

Chapter Thirty-Five
The Right Weight for October

Mother surprised me Sunday evening.

I was at the kitchen counter waiting for the printer to finish a set of ingredient sheets. It had taken over the space beside the bread box that afternoon, and each time it pulled in another page, it gave a small mechanical shudder that made the bread-box lid tremble. Across the table, supplier information lay beneath a volunteer schedule, held flat by the saltshaker.

My vacation began in the morning.

So far, it looked remarkably like paperwork.

Tom had gone out to put away his tools. Upstairs, Molly was moving around her room, opening drawers and calling down occasional questions about things she believed I might have washed. I had already found her missing rehearsal shirt on the back of a dining-room chair. I was holding it in one hand when Mother’s name appeared on my phone.

For several seconds, I let it ring.

Our last conversation had ended politely. That was part of the trouble. I knew what to do when Mother was openly irritated with me. Her careful kindness on Friday had left me with nothing to answer and no way to stop thinking about the hotel photograph.

I draped Molly’s shirt over my shoulder and picked up the phone.

“Hello?”

“I’ve been thinking.”

Mother rarely announced that unless the thinking had produced a decision affecting other people.

“About what?”

“This band-camp situation.”

A page slid into the printer tray. I lifted it, checked the heading, and placed it on the wrong pile.

“It’s handled.”

“Clearly not, if you’ve surrendered an entire week of vacation.”

“I didn’t surrender it.”

“What would you call it?”

“I’m using it.”

“Not in Greenville.”

I straightened the papers in front of me, tapping their edges against the counter until they were perfectly even.

“Mother—”

“I’m going to Nashville Monday.”

My hands stopped.

“What?”

“Macy’s is having an end-of-season sale.”

Of course it was. Somewhere between Bowling Green and Nashville, summer had apparently ended, though July remained fully committed to making Kentucky uninhabitable.

“At Green Hills?”

“Where else would I go?”

Bowling Green had stores. Mother had never considered that relevant.

She explained that she intended to arrive when Macy’s opened and leave before the worst of the afternoon traffic. There were certain departments she wanted to visit first. She had a coupon, although whether it applied to the sale merchandise remained a matter she intended to settle in person.

I rested my hip against the counter and waited. Behind me, the printer began another sheet.

“I can help Tuesday through Friday,” she said.

For a moment, I thought she meant helping me finish the preparations at home.

“With what?”

“Band camp, Lydia.”

I looked toward the table, at the empty spaces on the volunteer schedule.

“You want to come to the school?”

“Yes.”

“All four days?”

“That would be Tuesday through Friday.”

There was the familiar impatience, but nothing followed it. No observation about the trip. No reminder of what my decision had cost her.

I pulled out a chair and sat down, moving the saltshaker to make room for my elbow.

“What would you want to do?”

“Drinks. Put me at the drink table.”

“Tea and lemonade?”

“And water. I assume you are giving them water.”

“Yes, Mother.”

“Well, good.”

She sounded settled now, as though we had reached the useful portion of the conversation and could stop circling it.

I ran my thumb along the edge of the volunteer schedule. I had spent much of the afternoon wondering whom I could call without becoming the kind of person people avoided answering. Mother had been nowhere on my list.

“You don’t have to do this because of the trip,” I said.

“I know.”

“I mean it.”

“So do I.”

I leaned back. Overhead, Molly dropped something substantial onto her bedroom floor.

Mother heard it. “What was that?”

“Either her band bag or a piece of furniture. I’m choosing the band bag.”

“Does she need help getting ready?”

“She needs to discover that her belongings continue to exist when she isn’t looking at them.”

Mother made a small, approving sound. She had waited years for me to appreciate certain difficulties.

Then she grew quiet.

“I am still disappointed about Greenville,” she said.

“I know.”

“And our two nights in Gatlinburg. I had already bought a cardigan.”

I looked down at the shirt across my shoulder and picked a loose thread from its sleeve.

“For the balcony?”

“It was the right weight. Not one of those heavy things you have to take off as soon as you go inside.”

“What color?”

“A soft blue.”

I could see her in it, one hand closing the front against the morning air, her coffee in the other.

“Keep it,” I said.

“Oh, I intend to. It was an excellent price.”

I smiled, but my throat had tightened.

On her end, something clicked softly against a hard surface. A cup, perhaps, being set down.

“I don’t want you spending the whole week worn out and upset with everybody,” she said.

“You’ve decided to do this. Let me help you do it.”

I lowered my eyes to the schedule.

There were things I could have said about not being upset with everybody. Instead, I took the pen from beneath the saltshaker.

“I’d like that.”

“Then it’s settled.”

“Thank you, Mother.”

“You’re welcome, sweetheart.”

I pressed my lips together and took a slow breath through my nose before I trusted myself to say anything else.

I wrote June—drinks in Tuesday’s empty space. My handwriting came out smaller than the other names, and I went back over the J.

“You know Darla coordinates everything,” I said.

“I assumed somebody did.”

“She has a system for the tables and the supplies. I’ll need to check with her.”

“Of course. Give me her number, and I’ll find out what she needs.”

I paused, pen still touching the paper.

“What?” Mother asked.

“Nothing.”

“You stopped talking.”

“I’m adjusting to how agreeable this is.”

“Don’t make it difficult.”

I laughed, and heard the answering warmth in her voice when she asked how many people we fed.

“Usually around ninety, counting students, staff, and volunteers.”

“Usually?”

“It changes. People come to work on the props, or stay after helping with something.”

“And you feed them.”

“If they’re there.”

“All right,” she said. “Then we’ll allow for that.”

There was no disbelief in her voice now. She had spent too many years making sure nobody left her house hungry to require an explanation.

“I’ll start with six gallons of tea Tuesday,” she said. “Three sweet, two unsweet, and one lightly sweetened. Then we’ll see what they drink.”

“Lightly sweetened?”

“Some people want sweet tea but still enjoy pretending to be responsible.”

“That’s a lot of tea.”

“It’s a lot of people.”

“You don’t have to make it all yourself. We can buy—”

“Lydia. Everyone in Bowling Green knows I make the best sweet tea.”

I wasn’t going to argue with her about that.

Her tea had appeared at bridal showers and church receptions, funeral lunches and graduation parties. I had seen women take a sip, look thoughtfully into their glasses, and ask what she put in it.

“Enough sugar,” Mother always said.

I remembered the sound of her long spoon against a pitcher, the kitchen windows dark behind her while she prepared for some gathering the next day. She stirred the sugar into the tea while it was still hot, then added a thin curl of lemon peel that would be gone before anyone arrived.

On those evenings, I used to ask why she was still working when everybody else had gone to bed.

Now my printer was running on a Sunday night, and I had written three reminders about sandwiches.

“I’ll need to know what containers you have,” she said. “And whether there’s room to keep things cold.”

“There is. I’ll send you Darla’s number.”

“Read it to me. I have something to write on.”

I read the number slowly. Mother repeated it without an error.

“Don’t call her tonight,” I said.

“Why would I call her tonight?”

“Because you’re already holding a pen.”

For a moment, the line was quiet.

“I can hold a pen without using the telephone.”

“Mother.”

“I’ll call tomorrow.”

"After you shop.”

“Possibly while I shop. Sales involve waiting.”

I pictured her outside a fitting room at Green Hills, a linen jacket draped over one arm, asking Darla about refrigerator space while a sales associate tried to retrieve a hanger. For a moment I could hear the two of them discovering that the other had already made a list.

“Tell her you’re handling drinks,” I said. “Only drinks. You don’t need to take on anything else.”

“I’m offering four days, Lydia. I’m not applying for a position.”

“I’m beginning to understand how easily one becomes the other.”

“Well, you can explain it to me Tuesday.”

The printer fell quiet. From where I sat, I could see the finished sheets resting in the tray, one corner curled upward. There was still plenty to do, but for the first time that evening I did not immediately get up to do it.

“What time should I be there?” Mother asked.

“We’ll work that out with Darla tomorrow.”

“All right. And Lydia?”

“Yes?”

“We will go to Greenville.”

I laid the pen down.

“Mother, you don’t have to—”

“I know we can’t go in October the way we planned. But when this week is over, let’s look at the calendar. You and me.”

Through the window, I saw Tom crossing the yard toward the house. He paused at the back step to wipe his shoes, one hand resting against the doorframe.

“I’d like that,” I said.

“So would I.”

We stayed on the telephone for another moment. I could hear faint television voices in her house, far enough away that she must have left the room to call me.

Then she asked, “Are volunteers allowed to wear sandals?”

I looked beneath the table at my bare feet. A narrow red mark remained on one heel from Friday’s pumps.

“I’d wear your white tennis shoes. You’ll be standing a lot.”

“With the navy pants?”

“Those would be fine.”

“They’re comfortable, but they do need a better top.”

“You’ll be at Macy’s tomorrow.”

“That had occurred to me.”

When we hung up, I remained at the table. Then I drew the schedule closer and added her name beneath Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday.

Upstairs, Molly called to ask whether I had found her shirt.

“It’s down here,” I answered. “Come get it.”

I heard her door open, then her footsteps on the stairs. I folded the shirt while I waited, smoothing its shoulders with my hands.

The ingredient sheets could stay in the printer a little longer.

Chapter Thirty-Six
June’s Sweet Tea

By the following Tuesday, hospitality was no longer consuming my lunch break.

It had consumed my vacation.

I arrived at Cumberland Hills shortly after eight with the printed ingredient sheets, an ice schedule, three boxes of serving gloves, and enough plastic wrap to preserve the entire percussion section.

The cafeteria tables had been arranged before I arrived.

The colored labels waited in their plastic holders. The football players still watched from between the serving-line doors.

Above them, the old sign continued asking heaven to protect the team on Friday night.

I wondered how many prayers it took to keep a marching band safe through an entire season.

Darla had left a yellow adhesive note on the drink table.

CHECK ICE AT 10:00.

She had used words.

Progress did not always announce itself with a trumpet fanfare.

Sometimes it appeared on a yellow adhesive note in block letters.

At nine-twenty, the cafeteria door opened, and Mother entered carrying two large insulated beverage dispensers.

She wore white ankle-length pants, a sleeveless royal-blue blouse, gold hoop earrings, and tan leather sandals entirely too attractive for a public-school cafeteria. Her silver-blonde hair remained perfectly arranged despite the late July humidity.

So did her coral lipstick.

She had tied a white apron around her waist. Blue piping bordered the edges, and a small embroidered lemon decorated one pocket. Across the front, stitched in blue script, were the words:

JUNE’S SWEET TEA

I had never seen the apron before.

I suspected it had been purchased the day before at Green Hills Mall and carried directly to a bridal shop somewhere in Nashville to have JUNE’S SWEET TEA embroidered across the front.

At her own bridal shop, Mother had been known for having the married names of certain brides embroidered inside the linings of their gowns, along with their wedding dates. She did not offer this to every bride. Only the ones she particularly liked—or the ones whose mothers had behaved well enough to deserve it.

For years, brides had lifted layers of satin on their anniversaries and found their new names hidden inside, stitched there by a woman who understood that marriage changed more than what appeared on the invitations.

Mother would have known exactly which Nashville shop could embroider an apron before closing time.

She would also have known that waiting for it gave her an excellent reason to spend another hour at Green Hills Mall.

I did not ask.

Some knowledge arrived only when Mother decided the world was prepared to receive it.

Asking questions merely delayed the announcement.

Behind her came Tom carrying two more dispensers.

“You recruited my husband?”

“He has a truck.”

Tom gave me an apologetic look.

“She called at seven-fifteen.”

“I needed him at seven-thirty,” Mother said.

“I was informed of that.”

Mother directed him toward the drink table with two fingers and the serene authority of a woman who had spent more than thirty years guiding nervous brides, grieving widows, and poorly prepared hostesses through occasions they had mistakenly believed they were capable of managing alone.

Tom obeyed.

Mother set down her dispensers and smoothed the front of her apron.

Only then did I notice that each dispenser wore a fitted cover made from blue-and-white striped cotton. The spouts extended through neatly bound openings, and each lid had been topped with a matching circle of fabric.

Even Mother’s beverage dispensers had arrived appropriately dressed.

“Where did you get the covers?” I asked.

“I made them.”

“All six?”

“I didn’t pull down the green velvet draperies at Tara, Lydia. It’s washable cotton I bought at Joann’s before they went out of business.”

“You still had that much?”

“I planned ahead.”

Of course she had.

Mother considered an eight-yard remnant neither clutter nor an indulgence. It was preparedness for a need the rest of us had not yet developed the foresight to anticipate.

She lifted the cover from one of the unsweet dispensers and turned the container slightly.

Near the bottom was a dark crescent-shaped mark.

“That came from Buzzy Newton’s shoe at his first wedding. That was in 2008. His first wife was Beverly Schriever, from Somerset. Rather homely, but she had beautiful manners and came from money. Her grandfather held the patent on the little plastic zipper they put on freezer bags.”

“Mother.”

“What? Beverly knew she wasn’t pretty. That was why she developed such lovely manners.”

“Mother.”

“Like I was saying, Buzzy went through most of her inheritance in four months. What remained got him elected to the state House in Frankfort.”

“Mother.”

“I am trying to explain the mark, Lydia.”

She pointed toward the bottom of the dispenser.

“Buzzy knocked it over because the only way that man could make it through the ceremony was to stay buzzed.”

Tom leaned closer to examine the mark.

“They named him Buzzy?”

“His Christian name was Eugene. Buzzy was more honest.”

“What happened to the tea?”

“Most of it landed on Beverly’s mother.”

Tom looked at Mother.

“Did that delay the wedding?”

“It should have.”

Mother pulled the fitted cover back into place, concealing the mark.

“The marriage didn’t last six months.”

“You’ve kept the dispenser all this time?” I asked.

Mother looked at me as though I had suggested discarding a family heirloom.

“The dispenser was perfectly good.”

The cafeteria door opened again.

Darla came through carrying her clipboard. She wore her fluorescent safety vest, radio, two pairs of reading glasses, and three Sharpies. A fourth Sharpie had been clipped into her hair.

Mother turned toward me and spoke only with her eyes and the careful movement of her lips.

I had been reading those silent lips since I was four.

What in God’s name is she wearing?

Darla stopped when she saw Mother.

“You’re June?”

Mother smiled.

“I am.”

“I’m Darla Hensley.”

“I know. Lydia described you to a tee.”

Mother allowed her eyes to rest briefly on the fluorescent vest.

“Although she failed to mention how eye-catching your style is.”

Darla looked down at herself.

“It’s a safety vest.”

“Yes,” Mother said. “It certainly is.”

They stood facing one another.

Darla wore fluorescent yellow, a radio, and enough writing instruments to conduct an inventory of the National Guard.

Mother wore gold hoops, coral lipstick, and the untroubled confidence of a woman who believed good manners could conceal almost any act of aggression.

I watched two highly organized systems recognize one another.

Darla pointed toward a folding table near the wall.

“That’s the drink station.”

Mother looked at it.

Then she looked toward the serving lines.

“No.”

Darla lowered her clipboard.

“The table?”

“The location.”

“We put drinks there yesterday.”

“That explains why the floor is sticky.”

Darla looked at the floor.

Mother pointed toward a longer table near the cafeteria exit.

“Students should collect their drinks after they receive their food. Otherwise, they’ll carry cups through the serving line, set them down, forget which drink belongs to them, and knock over at least three before lunch ends.”

“They didn’t knock over three yesterday.”

“How many?”

Darla hesitated.

“Two.”

“And today there will be no more spills.”

Darla studied the proposed location.

Mother continued.

“The sweet tea needs to be farthest from the doors. Water nearest. Lemonade in the middle.”

“Why?” Darla asked.

“Students who want water make decisions quickly. Students who want sweet tea stand there as though selecting a life partner.”

“What about unsweet tea?”

“Those people have already accepted disappointment. They won’t hold up the line.”

“Mother.”

“I make excellent unsweet tea. That doesn’t mean anybody is happy about choosing it.”

Tom turned away.

His shoulders moved once.

Darla examined the path students would follow.

I could see her resisting.

I could also see her calculating.

Mother waited without speaking. She had learned years earlier that silence was most useful after telling someone what needed to be done.

Darla called to two prop fathers.

“We’re moving the drink station.”

Mother smiled.

Not triumphantly.

That would have been unnecessary.

Tom and the fathers moved the table.

Mother watched until it was exactly where she wanted it.

Then she looked at Tom.

“The flowers are still in your truck.”

Darla turned toward her.

“The flowers?”

“A table needs something living on it.”

“Teenagers need room to get their drinks.”

“That is why I brought a small arrangement.”

Tom returned carrying a blue-and-white china pitcher filled with white hydrangeas from Mother’s yard. The blossoms had been cut low and arranged tightly, with none of the stems showing.

Mother placed the pitcher at the far end of the table, turned it a quarter inch, and stepped back.

Darla studied it.

“This is a drink station.”

“Yes,” Mother said. “That is why I didn’t bring the tall arrangement.”

Darla looked at the hydrangeas again.

“They are pretty.”

“People behave better around fresh flowers.”

“Teenagers?”

“No,” Mother said. “But one should continue to offer them opportunities.”

Mother opened a large canvas bag covered in blue hydrangeas and began removing supplies: cream-colored labels, clear plastic holders, stacks of cups, lemon-printed cocktail napkins, three long-handled spoons, a cutting board, a small knife wrapped in a dish towel, two cucumbers, a container of lemon wedges, and a pair of silver serving tongs.

Darla watched the collection grow.

“You brought your own tongs?”

“I didn’t know what would be available.”

“We have tongs.”

“I see that.”

Mother looked toward the cafeteria utensils.

“I’m sure they’ve served you faithfully.”

She set her silver tongs beside the lemons.

Darla pointed toward the cucumbers.

“What are those for?”

“One dispenser will have cucumber water.”

Darla looked at the cucumbers.

“Do teenagers drink cucumber water?”

“Not yet.” Mother lifted one and examined it. “But today is as good a day as any to improve their palates. Cucumber water has class, and bless their hearts, Bowling Green’s young people could use a little more of it.”

She placed the cucumber beside the cutting board.

“They’re playing Copland, aren’t they?”

“Part of the show,” I said.

“Then they’re old enough for cucumber water. Classical taste should not end at the ears.”

Darla looked from Mother to the cucumber.

“With the whole band?”

“You have to begin somewhere.”

Mother began arranging the dispensers.

“Three sweet teas,” Darla said, consulting her clipboard.

“Three sweet. Two unsweet. One lightly sweetened.”

Darla looked up.

“What’s lightly sweetened?”

“For people who want sweet tea but enjoy believing they possess self-control.”

Darla pointed to the first position on the table.

“That one goes here.”

“Last.”

“First keeps students moving.”

“Last keeps them from mistaking it for the good tea.”

They stared at one another.

I stepped between them.

“Perhaps it could go in the middle.”

Neither woman answered.

The lightly sweetened tea eventually went in the middle.

International treaties had been negotiated with less effort.

Mother placed her handwritten labels in front of the dispensers. Each had been lettered in blue ink on cream-colored card stock with a thin blue border.

Darla picked one up.

“These aren’t waterproof.”

Mother reached into the hydrangea bag and produced six clear plastic holders.

Darla looked at them.

Then at Mother.

Something passed between the two women.

Respect, perhaps.

Or the beginning of a dangerous alliance.

Mother sliced the cucumbers into thin, even circles. She dropped them into the water dispenser one at a time, pausing after every few slices to judge their distribution.

“You know they all float to the top,” Darla said.

“Only if you allow them to.”

Darla watched Mother stir the water with one of her long-handled spoons.

“I suppose that looks nice.”

“Thank you.”

“I didn’t say it was necessary.”

“No one ever does at first.”

Mother arranged the lemon wedges with the silver tongs, stepped back, and surveyed the completed drink station.

Then she reached into the lemon-trimmed pocket of her apron and removed a small gold compact.

She opened it and examined her reflection.

Darla watched her.

Mother pressed one finger lightly beneath the corner of her mouth, studied her lipstick, and decided no repair was required. She closed the compact and returned it to the pocket beside a gold tube of coral lipstick.

I had watched her perform that same inspection before church, weddings, funerals, parent-teacher conferences, and one emergency-room visit involving my brother and a fishhook.

Mother did not believe vanity excused disorder.

She believed a woman should be prepared to face disorder properly.

Tom finished positioning the cups.

Mother looked at him.

“That’s exactly right.”

“Thank you, June.”

“You’re a good man, Tom.”

He smiled.

“I appreciate that.”

She glanced at me.

“Someone should tell you more often.”

There it was.

Mother could deliver affection and criticism in the same sentence without spilling either one.

I looked toward the cucumber water.

She was still disappointed in me.

She also loved me.

Both things were true, and Mother had never considered truth obligated to simplify itself for anyone’s comfort.

She had come because I had given away our week. Because another woman was sitting in a Louisville hospital beside her injured mother. Because ninety students would soon be hot and thirsty.

And because Molly looked enough like Mother at fourteen to satisfy Mother’s long-standing hope that someone in the family might eventually take after her.

Mother tied her apron more securely.

“Now,” she said, “where are the lemons?”

Darla pointed toward the refrigerator.

“I thought you brought lemons.”

“I brought enough for the tea. I haven’t seen what you bought.”

“They’re in the bottom produce drawer.”

“How many?”

“Four bags.”

“What size bags?”

Darla looked at her clipboard.

Mother waited.

“Two-pound.”

“Seeded or seedless?”

Darla lowered the clipboard.

“They’re lemons.”

Mother smiled warmly.

“We’ll look together.”

Darla did not move for a moment.

Then she placed her clipboard on the table and led Mother toward the refrigerator.

They walked side by side, discussing ice quantities, cup sizes, and whether the lemonade required its own wastebasket.

Mother had been inside Cumberland Hills for less than twenty minutes.

The drink station had been relocated. The sweet tea had been classified according to moral seriousness. The hydrangeas had begun encouraging better behavior. The cucumber water had started improving the classical taste of Bowling Green’s youth, and the lemons were about to undergo inspection.

Darla had found an equal.

Or at least Darla appeared to think she had.

My mother had entered the Cumberland Hills Band Boosters.

Civilization would not be the same.

Chapter Thirty-Eight
While Nothing Important Was Happening

Music drifted into the cafeteria shortly before ten.

It came from the band room down the hall, carried through doors propped open between the building and the practice field. With school still two weeks away, there were no bells or crowded hallways to stop it.

Not the band.


A recording of a symphony orchestra.

The first chord was so quiet that I mistook it for air moving through the building.

Then another appeared.

The music did not announce itself.

It gathered.

For a moment, the stainless-steel serving lines, brown floor tiles, and folding tables receded.

I saw open land.

A white house.

Morning light moving across an empty floor.

“And it means lunch is at eleven-thirty.”

Some of us heard what we had lost.

Chapter Thirty-Nine
You’ve Been Playing the Notes

By Tuesday afternoon, all sixty-seven band students were sitting on the rehearsal-room floor.

They occupied the space in loose, uneven rows, their backs against chairs, walls, instrument cases, or one another. Water jugs stood between their knees. A trumpet player had folded his rehearsal shirt into a pillow and placed it behind his head. Near the rear of the room, the percussionists had arranged themselves with the suspicious comfort of people who had claimed territory before anyone else understood territory was available.

The lights were off.

Sunlight entered through the narrow windows high along one wall and laid pale rectangles across the carpet. Dust drifted slowly inside them, disturbed whenever a student shifted or reached for water.

A recording played softly through the rehearsal-room speakers. Strings moved beneath a melody that had not yet revealed where it intended to go.

On the screen, the projector displayed an empty wooden room.

White walls.

A plain table.

Two ladder-back chairs.

There was nothing decorative and nothing unnecessary. Light fell across the bare floor, stopping just short of the room’s shadowed corners.

Mercer stood beside the podium with his arms folded. Nia leaned against the front wall, one ankle crossed over the other. Her posture appeared relaxed, but nothing about Nia Brooks ever suggested inattention.

Three Western students sat near the door—the same young teachers I had seen watching rehearsals beside the practice field. Their notebooks rested open across their knees.

A fourth person sat with them.

She appeared to be in her early sixties, tall even while seated, with long limbs folded comfortably beneath a pair of wide-legged black trousers. Her silver hair was brushed straight back from a high forehead and secured at the nape of her neck with a tortoiseshell clasp.

She had high cheekbones, a firm, expressive mouth, and the sort of angular beauty that age had clarified rather than diminished. Something about her reminded me of Katharine Hepburn—not the movie-star glamour, but the intelligence in the face and the faint suggestion that she had already considered most conventional expectations and found them unnecessary.

An oatmeal-colored linen jacket hung open over a crisp white shirt with the collar turned up slightly at the back. Red-framed glasses rested low on her nose. A wide hammered-silver cuff circled one wrist. In her other hand, she held a sharpened pencil, turning it slowly between her fingers while she listened.

Her sandals were dark red leather, the same deliberate shade as her glasses.

A canvas WKU bag stood beside her feet, crowded with scores, manuscript paper, and loose pages marked in several colors of ink.

She had not spoken since we entered.

She had not needed to.

The room had already made space for her.

I remained near the doorway.

Mother stood beside me with a cup of sweet tea held in one hand. She wore the navy pants we had discussed Sunday evening, but she had rejected my recommendation about the white tennis shoes. Instead, a pair of flat leather sandals revealed toenails painted the soft coral color she wore every summer.

She caught me looking at them.

“My feet are perfectly comfortable.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You looked.”

Mother had always been able to hear certain kinds of criticism before I expressed them.

Nobody asked us to leave. By then, I had learned that adults carrying food or beverages were permitted to enter almost anywhere during band camp. Mother’s sweet tea apparently gave her the same authority as a faculty identification badge.

The recording reached a brighter passage.

Brass entered, warm and spacious. The sound grew until it seemed too large for the rehearsal room, yet it never felt crowded. Beneath it, the strings moved with a quiet expectancy, as though something were approaching from beyond the walls of the empty room on the screen.

Mother stopped lifting the cup toward her mouth.

Mercer ended the recording.

The silence afterward felt unusually complete. Even the students who had been adjusting their positions became still.

He looked across the room.

“What did you hear?”

No one volunteered.

Teenagers who could maintain six simultaneous conversations across a cafeteria became devoted to silence when an adult asked a question without one correct answer.

Mercer waited.

He did not repeat himself or offer them choices. He simply allowed the question to remain in the room until the silence became more uncomfortable than participation.

Eventually, a guard member raised her hand halfway.

“It sounds old.”

Mercer nodded. “What else?”

“Like a movie,” someone said.

“What kind of movie?”

“A farm movie.”

Several students laughed. The boy who had answered smiled with them until Mercer tilted his head.

“What makes it sound like a farm?”

The smile faded. He had not expected his answer to require evidence.

“I don’t know. It just does.”

“Good.”

That surprised everyone.

Mercer turned toward the whiteboard and wrote:

OPEN

Beneath it, he added:

DISTANT

Then:

BEGINNING

He placed the marker against the tray and faced the students again.

“What else?”

Molly raised her hand.

“Like morning.”

Mercer considered the answer and added MORNING to the list.

Molly lowered her hand with the quiet satisfaction of someone whose contribution had survived inspection.

Sam sat two students away with one knee raised and his arms folded across it. His expression suggested he had identified several weaknesses in the exercise and was deciding whether sharing them would improve his circumstances.

Mercer noticed.

“Rahman?”

Sam looked up. “What?”

Nia’s eyebrow rose.

Mercer’s expression did not change.

“What did you hear?”

Sam looked toward the empty room on the screen. He unfolded his arms and rested one hand against the carpet.

“Space.”

Mercer wrote it on the board.

“Good.”

“Too much of it,” Sam added.

Molly turned toward him.

Sam shrugged. “It takes a long time to get anywhere.”

“That’s the point,” she whispered.

“How do you know?”

“Because it sounds like it.”

“That was your answer.”

Mercer waited until they remembered he could hear them.

Then he turned to the board and wrote one final word.

HOME

Six words stood beneath one another:

OPEN

DISTANT

BEGINNING

MORNING

SPACE

HOME

“This is the first music in our show,” he said.

A student near the back raised his hand.

“We’ve already been playing the first music.”

“You’ve been playing the notes.”

Something changed in the room.

It was not dramatic. No one sat suddenly straighter. But several students looked again at the empty room on the screen. Molly lowered her eyes as though reconsidering something she had believed she already understood.

Mercer faced the image.

“The piece is Appalachian Spring by Aaron Copland.”

The woman in the oatmeal-colored jacket stood.

Mercer introduced her as Dr. Miriam Bell, professor of composition and music theory at Western Kentucky University.

“She was one of my teachers,” he said.

That changed Mercer slightly in the students’ eyes.

Band directors belonged to a category teenagers rarely connected with ordinary human development. The discovery that Mercer had once been a student suggested he might also have forgotten pencils, missed entrances, or arrived somewhere without the correct shoes.

Dr. Bell approached the podium. Her dark-red sandals made almost no sound against the floor.

“Mr. Mercer was an excellent student.”

Mercer looked at her.

Dr. Bell allowed the sentence to rest while she regarded him over the top of her glasses.

“Eventually.”

Laughter moved across the room. Even Nia smiled.

Mercer lowered his head briefly, and for a second he looked closer to thirty-six than he usually allowed himself to appear.

Dr. Bell restarted the recording at the beginning. She listened with her head tilted slightly, the pencil resting against her lower lip. After several measures, she lowered the volume.

“Appalachian Spring was written as a ballet for the choreographer Martha Graham,” she said. “Its story centers on a young couple beginning married life in rural Pennsylvania. There is a farmhouse, a community, hope, uncertainty, and the private fear that what they are building might not endure.”

The image on the screen changed to a farmhouse surrounded by open land.

“The title came after most of the music had already been composed. Copland did not begin with a plan to describe springtime in Appalachia. Martha Graham chose the title from a poem.”

Sam sat forward. His forehead tightened.

“So people hear mountains because of the title?”

“In part,” Dr. Bell said.

“Even though that wasn’t what he wrote?”

“In part.”

“That seems dishonest.”

A few students laughed.

Dr. Bell did not.

She looked over her glasses at Sam—not disapprovingly, but with the interest of someone who had finally been asked a useful question.

“It tells us that listeners help create meaning.”

Sam glanced toward the screen. “Even if the meaning wasn’t there when he wrote it?”

“Meaning does not stop with the composer.”

Dr. Bell stepped away from the podium.

“Once music enters the world, the person who wrote it is no longer the only person who decides what it means. Listeners bring their own memories to it. Their own hopes. Their own losses. A piece of music can begin in one place and become large enough to hold people who have never been there.”

Sam did not appear convinced.

But he was listening.

So was Mother.

Dr. Bell restarted the recording farther into the work.

A melody emerged.

Simple.

Balanced.

Familiar.

I knew it before I remembered its name.

The tune seemed smaller than the music surrounding it, yet everything changed when it arrived. The sound became warmer. More intimate. The empty room on the screen no longer appeared abandoned.

It seemed to be waiting for someone.

Dr. Bell stopped the recording.

“Who recognizes that?”

Several hands rose. A clarinet player near Molly identified it as “Simple Gifts.”

Dr. Bell nodded.

“It is a nineteenth-century Shaker tune written by Joseph Brackett. Through Copland’s use of it in Appalachian Spring, the melody became closely associated with American ideas of simplicity, community, renewal, and home.”

Tyler raised his hand.

“Were there Shakers in Kentucky?”

“There were.”

“Like the furniture people?”

“They made furniture,” Dr. Bell said. “They were also a religious community.”

Marcus leaned toward him.

“You thought they were a furniture company?”

“I knew there were chairs.”

“There are chairs everywhere.”

“Not Shaker chairs.”

Nia raised one eyebrow.

The discussion of American furniture ended.

A large brick building appeared beyond a broad green lawn. Tall windows crossed its face in measured rows. The structure was plain without seeming severe. Its proportions felt calm, as though every line had been considered and nothing needed to call attention to itself.

“This is South Union Shaker Village,” she said. “Near Auburn, west of Bowling Green.”

The room reacted differently.

Students knew Auburn. Some had driven through it on their way to somewhere else. Several had visited South Union on elementary-school field trips. A guard member said her cousin had been married at the old hotel.

The nearness altered the meaning.

The Shakers were no longer people who belonged to an abstract chapter of American history. They had planted crops beneath the same Kentucky sky these students complained about each morning. They had worked, worshiped, prepared meals, composed music, buried people they loved, and tried to make a home only a short drive from the roads we traveled every day.

Chapter Forty
I Usually Leave with Less

Dr. Bell looked across the students.

“How many of you have visited?”

Several hands rose, most of them slowly.

Mother lifted hers.

I turned toward her.

At first, I assumed she was remembering an elementary-school trip with me, although I had no memory of visiting South Union together. Then I noticed the way she was looking at the photograph.

It was not the expression of someone remembering a place she had visited.

It was the expression of someone seeing a part of her life she had never shown me.

Dr. Bell noticed her hand.

“You’ve been to South Union?”

“Many times,” Mother said.

I looked at her. “How many times?”

Her eyes moved briefly toward me.

“I go nearly every week.”

The answer was so unexpected that for a moment I wondered whether I had misunderstood her.

“You go every week?”

“Not every week. If the weather is bad, I stay home.”

Mother said this as though the weather were the only part of the answer requiring clarification.

“How long have you been doing that?”

She moved one thumb slowly along the side of her cup.

“About three years.”

I stared at her.

For three years, Mother had been getting into her car and driving west toward South Union. During those same years, I had spoken to her several times a week. I knew when she went to Green Hills. I knew when she had a dentist appointment and when someone from church needed a covered dish.

I had not known this.

“What do you do there?” Molly asked from the floor.

Mother found her among the clarinets.

“I walk the grounds.”

“In those shoes?” I asked before I could stop myself.

Mother looked down at her sandals.

“I own other shoes, Lydia.”

A few students smiled.

Then she turned back toward the image.

“Sometimes I walk,” she said. “Sometimes I sit in the field.”

Her voice had grown quieter.

“There’s a place beyond the buildings where you can see the land open in front of you. No traffic. No telephone. Nothing asking to be answered immediately.”

Dr. Bell lowered the pencil in her hand.

“It can be a very peaceful place,” she said.

“It is.”

Mother held the cup with both hands now.

“I pray there. Sometimes I meditate. Sometimes I just sit until whatever has been following me grows quiet enough for me to understand it.”

No one laughed.

Even Tyler watched her.

Molly leaned forward slightly. “What follows you?”

“Molly,” I said.

Mother glanced at me.

“It’s all right.”

For a moment, she seemed to consider how much of herself she intended to place in the room. Her shoulders remained straight, but her fingers tightened against the plastic cup.

“There are things I wish I had said differently,” she said. “Questions I should have asked sooner. Times I was so certain I knew what was best that I didn’t consider what being right might cost someone else.”

Her eyes met mine.

Only for a moment.

It was long enough.

I felt something shift beneath my ribs.

I had spent years believing Mother’s certainty protected her from regret. I had mistaken her refusal to discuss the past for an absence of feeling. All this time, while I waited for her to acknowledge what had happened between us, she had been carrying something of her own into a field west of Bowling Green.

Week after week.

Alone.

“What happens when you leave?” Molly asked.

Mother’s mouth softened.

“I usually leave with less than I brought.”

Dr. Bell regarded her with a quiet recognition that made me wonder whether she, too, had a place where she went to put down what she could no longer carry.

“That sounds like a gift,” she said.

Mother looked at the image for another moment.

“It has been a gift to me.”

She lifted her tea and took a small sip.

That was all.

No one applauded her honesty or asked her to explain which regrets she meant. Mercer did not turn the moment into a lesson. The students simply allowed Mother’s words to remain among them.

I was grateful for that.

I was not ready to know whether she had been speaking about me.

I was even less ready to know she had not.

Chapter Forty-One
Remaining in the Room

Dr. Bell changed the image. A long interior room appeared, its pale walls lined with windows. Wooden furniture had been arranged with such care that nothing seemed accidental.

Somewhere beneath her next words, the Shaker melody remained in my mind—plain, unadorned, and somehow spacious enough to hold what Mother had said.

“The South Union community was established early in the nineteenth century,” Dr. Bell said. “Its members farmed, worshiped, made furniture, prepared meals, produced goods, composed music, and arranged their lives around shared work.”

She pointed toward the photograph with her pencil.

“They believed the things they made should be useful. They valued careful work. Simplicity did not mean carelessness or emptiness. It meant removing what did not serve the purpose.”

Mercer looked toward the image.

Then he faced the band.

“Why do we have a window in the opener?”

No one answered.

He looked toward the clarinets.

“Molly?”

She straightened. A trace of her grandmother’s words remained in her expression.

“Because it’s part of the house?”

Her mouth pressed together.

“Better.”

“There it is.”

The sentence settled over the room.

I thought of Mother.

Not Mother standing beside me with coral toenails and a cup of sweet tea.

Mother alone in a field at South Union, sitting with the things she could not say anywhere else.

I thought of the trophy case.

Of Tom waiting beside a closed door he had eventually stopped knocking upon.

Of Layla standing in a half-unpacked kitchen, wondering what the move to Kentucky had given everyone except Amir.

Remaining in the room had become impossible.

Perhaps that was why some people left.

Perhaps it was also why others finally learned to move a wall.

Mercer changed the slide.

The screen now showed the Cumberland Hills field from above. Blue rectangles represented rolling walls. Smaller shapes marked windows, the porch, and the kitchen table.

The production looked more complicated on paper than it did from the parking lot.

Perhaps everything did when viewed from far enough above.

Behind me, Darla became very still.

Band boosters had not spent July constructing things so a director could discover in August that they represented artistic excess.

The hand lowered.

“Simple is not the same as easy.”

Chapter Forty-Two
When You Hear Him Leave

By the time the band moved outside, the morning had surrendered completely to July.

Heat lay across the practice field in visible layers, rising from the pavement and trembling above the patches of bare earth. The sky had cleared to such an unbroken blue that it appeared almost hard, and the sun pressed against every uncovered surface.

The grass along the sideline remained green in scattered places. Farther onto the field, it had been crossed so often that it had faded to something closer to straw. Dust rose around the students’ shoes and settled against their damp ankles. Water jugs stood in a long, uneven row, sweating dark circles into the ground.

I had worn Skechers instead of my office shoes, but even through their thick rubber soles I could feel the heat rising from the pavement. By the time I reached the sideline, the back of my shirt had begun to cling between my shoulder blades.

The rolling walls traveled badly over grass. Their wheels caught in shallow ruts, and the metal handles had absorbed enough sunlight to make the students release them quickly and shake their hands.

Darla appeared with a box of work gloves.

No one had asked her to bring them.

No one was surprised that she had.

Mercer climbed the rehearsal tower while Nia moved among the students below, correcting intervals and directing them toward their opening coordinates. The rolling walls stood between sections of the band, dividing the field into rooms that existed only when viewed from the front.

Window Two had been moved four feet closer to the front sideline.

I knew it was Window Two because Darla had written the number across the back in permanent marker large enough to be read by approaching aircraft.

The porch waited near Side One.

Tom stood with one hand pressed against the railing while Amir crouched beneath it, examining the brace from an angle apparently unavailable to anyone who remained standing. They were both pointing toward the same connection and explaining why the other man did not completely understand it.

Layla watched for several seconds before stepping away.

“They enjoy disagreeing,” she said.

I looked at the two men. “I believe the porch is incidental.”

The corners of Layla’s mouth lifted.

Across the field, the students began settling into place. Individual conversations faded. Instrument bells rose. The wall near Sam cast a narrow shadow across his shoulder before the student holding it adjusted the angle.

From the tower, Mercer surveyed the opening set. Nia moved to the front sideline and raised one hand.

The opening chord rose into the heat.

Outside, the sound was thinner than it had been in the rehearsal room. Four walls and a ceiling had protected it there. The room held each note close and returned part of it to the students who had created it.

The field kept nothing.

Every note had to cross the open air knowing it might disappear before reaching anyone.

The trumpet entrance spread across the field, bright but not yet secure. Low brass gathered beneath it, giving the sound weight. Woodwinds began moving between the walls, their feet bending the dry grass in straight, temporary lines.

Near the center of the field, Sam stepped away from the saxophone section.

The wall beside him moved too soon.

Its wheels struck a bare patch of earth and sent up a small cloud of dust. For an instant, it rolled directly in front of Sam and concealed half his body from the tower.

Mercer cut off the band.

The sound vanished, leaving only the electronic click of the metronome traveling across the empty field.

“Reset.”

Students lowered their instruments. Shoulders sagged beneath damp shirts. Several faces turned instinctively toward the row of water jugs before remembering that no break had been called.

They retraced their steps. Parent volunteers pulled the wall back to its starting position, its wheels bouncing over the uneven ground.

Nia raised her hand again.

The opening chord returned.

This time, Sam began his phrase before the wall moved. The student holding the prop glanced toward the tower, realized he was late, and jerked it forward. One wheel twisted sideways. The wall shuddered and stopped.

Mercer lowered one hand.

The music ended.

“Why is it moving?”

No one answered.

The metronome continued clicking.

Mercer reached down and switched it off.

Without the amplified pulse, the field seemed suddenly larger. Cicadas called from the trees beyond the practice area. Somewhere near the school, a truck changed gears.

Mercer leaned against the tower railing.

“Why is the wall moving?”

The student assigned to it tightened both hands around the metal handle.

“Count thirty-two.”

“I know when it moves,” Mercer said. “I’m asking why.”

The student stared at the plywood as though hoping Darla’s enormous number might contain the answer.

On the other side of the wall, Sam adjusted the alto saxophone against his body.

“Because my phrase leaves the chord.”

Mercer looked toward him.

“Show them.”

Sam glanced around the field. “With what?”

“Your instrument would be traditional.”

Laughter moved through the band, small and grateful in the heat.

Sam lifted the saxophone. His fingers settled against the keys, but his expression was less certain than his answer had been. Questioning an idea from the rehearsal-room floor was one thing. Standing alone with an instrument while sixty-six other students waited was something else.

Mercer asked the brass to sustain the opening chord beneath him.

They raised their instruments.

The chord began unevenly, then gathered into something steady. Sam entered quietly inside the harmony. His sound belonged so completely to what surrounded it that, for the first few notes, it was difficult to separate the saxophone from the larger chord.

Then the line changed.

One note leaned beyond the harmony.

Another carried it farther.

The melody grew warmer as it moved away from everything that had supported it. By the end of the phrase, it had not rejected the chord. It had simply discovered somewhere the chord could not follow.

In the space opening around Sam’s melody, I heard things he could not have placed there intentionally: his family leaving Dearborn, Mother walking alone at South Union, and the sixteen-year-old version of me leaving a band room without knowing whether I had escaped something or abandoned it.

Mercer pointed toward the wall.

“Move when you hear him leave.”

The student looked across the plywood at Sam.

“Not on thirty-two?”

“You’ll still arrive on thirty-two,” Mercer said. “The count tells you when. His music tells you why.”

Something changed in the student’s face. His attention shifted away from the number written across the wall and toward Sam.

They reset.

This time, the student did not stare at his feet or look toward the tower. He watched Sam raise the saxophone, then lowered his eyes and listened.

The brass chord settled across the field.

Sam entered within it.

His phrase began to turn.

As the first note moved beyond the harmony, the wall began to roll.

It was not perfect. One corner dragged slightly, and the student pushing from behind had to shorten his steps to keep it straight.

But the movement had purpose.

The wall no longer looked like a piece of painted plywood being pushed across a football field. It seemed to respond to something it could no longer contain. By the time Sam completed the phrase, an opening had appeared exactly where the music needed it.

For the first time, the visual event felt inevitable.

A few yards away, Molly looked through the opening toward Sam. Her lips parted slightly.

The concentration in her face gave way to something quieter—not surprise exactly, but recognition.

She had heard it too.

Mercer climbed back onto the tower.

“Again.”

A collective breath passed through the students, half exhaustion and half acceptance.

They returned to their opening positions. Parents guided the wall back across the grass. Sam stepped behind it and disappeared.

Nia raised her hand.

The opening chord began again beneath the enormous Kentucky sky.

Beside Sam, the student placed both hands against the wall and waited for the music to give him a reason to move.

Chapter Forty-Three
Not a Single Spill

At eleven-thirty, the cafeteria doors opened and the students came through them in waves.

The quiet room filled almost instantly with overheated voices, dragging shoes, and the hollow knock of water jugs against chair legs. Faces were pink from the sun. Damp hair lay against foreheads and necks. Shirts had darkened between shoulder blades, and the smell of dry grass, sunscreen, and teenage perspiration arrived several seconds before the end of the line.

Six gallons of tea stood behind her in orderly rows. Three dispensers held sweet tea, two held unsweetened, and one held Mother’s lightly sweetened compromise for people who wanted Southern hospitality without fully accepting its consequences.

At the end of the table stood three more containers.

One held lemonade. Another contained ice water crowded with thin slices of cucumber, their pale-green edges drifting against the clear sides of the dispenser. The third held ordinary ice water beneath a handwritten label Mother had made herself:

PLAIN KENTUCKY WATER

Apparently, water required a place of origin once cucumber had entered the conversation.

Mother had placed the sweet tea closest to the students.

Darla had wanted it nearer the wall.

Mother had won.

The first student chose plain Kentucky water.

The second studied the cucumber slices with understandable suspicion.

“What’s in that?”

“Cucumber,” Mother said.

“That is generally where cucumber water is kept.”

His eyebrows rose.

He drank again.

“Why is this actually good?”

Three heads leaned in his direction.

Everyone who tasted it liked it.

Even the students who had approached the container prepared not to.

The plain Kentucky water continued to serve those who believed vegetables and beverages should maintain separate lives.

And not a single spill.

Darla had predicted three.

I looked across the station at her. She was watching the students with the concentration of someone who had not abandoned her forecast so much as postponed it.

Chapter Forty-Four
Something Had Returned

The storm arrived at three-sixteen.

For most of the afternoon, it had existed only as a dark line beyond the trees, far enough away that no one stopped rehearsing but close enough that Nia checked the weather radar between repetitions.

Then the wind changed.

The heat that had pressed against the field all day loosened at once. The temperature dropped sharply enough that students lifted their faces. Leaves along the tree line turned their pale undersides toward the sky, and a dry gust swept across the field, lifting dust around shoes and carrying it in low sheets toward the parking lot.

From the tower, Mercer looked west.

The clouds were no longer a line. They had risen into a dark wall, blue-black at the bottom and almost green where the light caught them.

Nia held up her phone. Whatever she showed him required no discussion.

The first thunder came low and distant, less a crack than a warning moving through the ground beneath us.

Mercer’s whistle cut across the field.

“Inside. Now.”

Rehearsal ended immediately.

Students who had required repeated instructions to adjust one step in a form moved with remarkable speed once lightning became part of the instructional environment. Instruments disappeared into cases. Electronics were unplugged and lifted from the ground. Music stands collapsed. Water jugs swung from wrists while students reached for anything they had been assigned to carry.

Parents seized the rolling walls and pushed them toward the building. Their wheels struck ruts, caught in the dry grass, and rattled across the pavement. Tom and Amir guided the porch toward the overhang, one pulling while the other watched the clouds and offered instructions neither had time to debate.

The first drops struck the pavement before we reached the doors.

They were large and widely spaced, darkening the concrete in circles the size of quarters. One landed against my forearm. Another struck the top of my head.

Then the sky opened.

Rain swept across the parking lot so quickly that the far row of cars disappeared. Water hammered the metal roof above the entrance and poured from the edges of the overhang in solid sheets. Thunder broke directly overhead, and several students screamed before disguising it as laughter.

For a few frantic minutes, the band hallway became a place where everything arrived at once.

Wet students pressed against instrument cases and rolling walls. Parents maneuvered equipment carts through doors that suddenly seemed too narrow. Water jugs collected beneath benches and against lockers. One section of the porch extended across the entrance to the percussion room until Tom and Amir turned it sideways and wedged it beneath a trophy banner.

The outside humidity entered with them and settled over the cooler air of the building. So did the smell of rain, wet grass, damp clothing, valve oil, and sixty-seven teenagers who had spent most of the day in July heat.

Shoes squeaked across the terrazzo. Water gathered beneath equipment carts and spread toward the baseboards. Someone near the percussion room began laughing and could not seem to stop. A trumpet played two bright notes before Nia looked in its direction and said, “No.”

The trumpet stopped.

I stepped aside to let two guard members carry a soaked equipment bag past me. Someone pushed a rolling wall toward the center of the hallway, and I moved again.

When the crowd finally stopped rearranging itself, I found that it had placed me beside the trophy case.

Not intentionally.

At least, not anyone else’s intention.

Rain moved in silver sheets across the windows behind me, but the hallway lights reflected cleanly against the glass. Inside, trophies rose in uneven rows: polished brass figures, wooden bases, silver cups, plaques engraved with dates that had once meant everything to the people who earned them.

The photographs hung above them.

Sixteen-year-old Lydia waited behind the glass.

She sat in the second row, third from the end, with a clarinet resting across her lap. Her posture was straighter than I remembered. Her hair curved beneath her chin in the cut Mother had insisted made me look older. Tiny silver earrings caught the photographer’s flash—the pair Daddy had given me for my sixteenth birthday.

The girl in the picture looked directly at the camera.

Her face still believed the future would happen according to schedule.

I leaned closer to the glass.

For a moment, the hallway around me receded. The wet shoes, the laughter, and the scrape of equipment faded beneath the rain.

Then my eyes moved to the photograph beside it.

1999

The championship band.

Students crowded together beneath a banner, some smiling, others caught with their mouths open in exhausted celebration. A trophy stood at the center, tall enough that two students had been required to hold it upright.

I searched their faces even though I knew better.

The absence remained.

It always would.

Nothing I did now could place me inside that picture. No amount of remembering could move my face into the empty year between the girl with the clarinet and the championship band she had not stayed to become part of.

Mother came to stand beside me, leaving only a few inches between us.

She followed my eyes toward the photographs.

For once, she said nothing.

She did not comment on the haircut. She did not remind me how much I had loved band or how hard she had worked to make it possible. She did not ask why I had never gone back.

She did not allow the year that still stood between us to enter the hallway before either of us was ready.

She simply remained beside me.

It was a kind of grace I might once have missed because it made no announcement when it arrived.

Molly passed in front of the case carrying her clarinet case close against her chest. Her braid had darkened from the rain, and drops of water gathered at the end before falling onto the back of her shirt.

Sam walked beside her with his alto saxophone case hanging from one shoulder and the narrow soprano case in his other hand. He leaned toward her, listening with the patient resistance of someone preparing to disagree.

“The wall moved before your phrase changed,” Molly said.

“It moved when my phrase changed.”

“He anticipated it.”

“That means he understood it.”

“That means he was early.”

Sam opened his mouth to object, but Molly’s expression told him she had already prepared her next argument.

Neither of them looked toward the photographs.

They were too occupied with the music they had just played and the versions of themselves they were still becoming.

For once, I did not want them to stop.

They disappeared into the rehearsal room, their disagreement continuing after they passed through the doorway.

The hallway held several Cumberland Hills bands at the same time.

There was the band behind the glass, preserved in photographs and polished trophies. There were the sixty-seven wet students crowding the present, their cases blocking doorways and their voices rising above the storm. And somewhere beyond both was the band Mercer believed they might become.

I had spent years thinking only one version could be true.

From inside the rehearsal room came the click of a clarinet case opening.

A few seconds later, someone began playing the “Simple Gifts” melody.

The sound was tentative at first. The opening notes emerged beneath the rain, paused, and started again.

I recognized Molly’s playing.

A mother does.

Perhaps not among sixty-seven instruments. Alone, yes. I knew the slight breath before she began and the way she leaned into a phrase when she was uncertain of it, as though determination might carry the note where confidence had not yet learned to go.

She played the melody more slowly than the recording Dr. Bell had shared that morning. Without accompaniment, it sounded smaller and more vulnerable.

A second clarinet joined her.

Then a third.

The rhythm steadied. The melody found support without losing its simplicity.

A trumpet entered from somewhere in the hallway, confidently beginning the melody a step too high.

Several students shouted at once.

The trumpet stopped. A few seconds later, it returned on the correct pitch and with considerably less confidence.

Near the instrument lockers, a trombone found the bass line. Someone inside the rehearsal room supplied another harmony. The notes traveled through the building without instruction, moving beneath the rain and between the voices of students still putting away equipment.

Mercer stood near his office with one hand resting against the doorframe.

He did not raise his hands or ask for attention. He did not tell the students where to enter or correct the trumpet player who continued arriving slightly early.

Nia looked toward him.

Mercer gave an almost invisible shake of his head.

Let them.

Students joined from different parts of the hallway.

They could not all see one another. Rolling walls stood between them, and the porch blocked part of a doorway. Some remained near the instrument lockers while others gathered inside the rehearsal room. A mellophone played from behind Window Two. Low brass entered near the water fountain. The trumpet player finally learned to wait.

Percussion added the pulse softly against a practice pad.

The music was not polished. The balance was terrible. Entrances arrived with more enthusiasm than agreement, and one sustained note wandered far enough from the pitch that several students turned toward it.

But the melody held.

It passed from one section to another until the entire hallway seemed to know it. The students were no longer playing because a director had told them what the music meant. They were answering one another across the walls.

Behind the glass, sixteen-year-old Lydia held her clarinet.

In front of it, Molly played hers.

Beside me stood the mother who had driven me to rehearsals, waited in parking lots, and altered bridal gowns late into the night while I practiced in the back room of her shop. She had listened through walls then, too. I had never considered how many times she must have stopped sewing when she recognized my sound.

I felt the years between us.

Not as distance.

As harmony.

One note existed because another came before it. That did not make them the same note, and it did not require either one to disappear for the other to be heard.

Rain struck the roof.

The students played.

For perhaps thirty seconds, the sound became fuller than sixty-seven people.

Not louder.

Larger.

There was a difference.

I looked toward Mother.

She was no longer watching the photographs. Her eyes had closed, and the expression on her face was the same quiet expression she had worn when the image of South Union appeared on the rehearsal-room screen.

I could not tell whether she was hearing Molly’s clarinet or remembering mine.

Then her hand moved between us.

For an instant, I thought she might take mine. Instead, her fingers rested lightly against the inside of my wrist, where the pulse moved close beneath the skin.

The gesture was so small that no one else would have noticed.

I did.

I did not turn my hand or reach for hers. Neither of us had become that brave.

I simply remained still and allowed her fingers to rest there.

Then Tyler dropped something metal near the percussion room.

The crash struck the hallway hard enough to break the spell.

Trumpets stopped. Someone laughed. The last clarinet note wavered and disappeared beneath another roll of thunder.

Mercer stepped away from his office.

“All right. Pack up and wait for the weather clearance.”

Cases opened. Conversations returned. Students began asking when they could leave and whether lightning still counted if they had not personally seen it.

The hallway became itself again.

But Mother’s fingers remained against my wrist for another second.

Something stayed after the music ended.

Or perhaps something had returned.

Chapter Forty-Five
Simple Gifts

The rain had softened by the time Molly, and I left.

What had hammered against the school roof now fell in a fine, steady mist, gathering along the edge of the overhang before dropping onto the pavement. Thunder still moved somewhere east of town, but it had become a distant sound—more memory than warning.

Mother had already departed in her Cadillac.

Before leaving, she announced that Wednesday would require more cucumber water, more lemon, and fewer opportunities for percussionists to refill sixteen-ounce cups.

Darla had agreed.

That concerned me.

Water followed the curb in narrow brown streams, carrying grass clippings, leaves, and one abandoned straw wrapper toward the storm drain. The parking lot reflected the security lights even though sunset remained several hours away. Cars appeared twice—once beneath the gray afternoon sky and again in the broken water below them.

The storm had cooled the air, but not enough to remove the humidity. It settled against my face and arms as we crossed the parking lot.

Molly carried her clarinet case and uniform bag. Her braid lay damp against the back of her shirt, and the bottom of one pant leg had darkened where she had stepped into a puddle.

I carried the empty label box, three unused markers, and a container of sliced apples no one had eaten because teenagers regarded fruit as a form of administrative punishment.

Halfway to the car, Molly shifted her clarinet case to her other hand and looked toward the western sky.

Beyond the retreating storm, a strip of pale-yellow light had opened above the trees. The clouds surrounding it remained dark, making the brightness appear less like sunset than a door someone had left open.

“Do you think we’ll ever go there?” she asked.

“Where?”

“South Union.”

“I’ve been.”

She stopped walking and turned toward me.

“When?”

“Fourth grade.”

“That doesn’t count.”

“Why not?”

“You probably don’t remember it.”

“I remember a staircase.”

“Was it at South Union?”

I considered the question.

“I don’t know.”

“Then you don’t remember South Union.”

Fourteen-year-olds were ruthless custodians of accuracy when the information belonged to other people.

We reached the car. Molly opened the rear door and placed her clarinet carefully across the seat before adding the uniform bag beside it. I set the apples on the floor and promised myself I would remember them when we reached home.

“Mr. Mercer says we might go sometime,” she said.

I looked over the roof of the car. “He said that?”

“He said maybe.”

Those were not the same statement, but I lacked the energy to begin another proceeding in the rain.

Molly climbed into the passenger seat. I got behind the wheel, placed the markers in the cup holder, and started the engine.

The air-conditioning came on too cold and too high. Molly turned the vent away from her face without asking, then leaned back against the seat.

For several minutes, we drove without speaking.

Wet tires whispered over the road. Steam rose from places where the rain had struck pavement still holding the day’s heat. Water shivered beneath passing cars and broke against the curb. With the windows closed, the world beyond the windshield seemed quieter than it was.

At the first stoplight, I lowered my window an inch.

The air smelled washed clean—wet earth, grass, hot asphalt, and something green released by the storm.

Beside me, Molly tapped her fingers against her knee.

Four even beats.

A pause.

Then the pattern began again.

I recognized the rhythm.

“Simple Gifts” had followed us into the car.

At the next light, Molly began humming part of the melody. There was no performance in it. She did not sit straighter or check whether I was listening. The tune simply appeared beneath her breath because it had remained inside her and needed somewhere to go.

I thought about the hallway.

The students had been separated by walls, doorways, equipment carts, and a section of porch. Most could not see everyone contributing to the sound. They had listened anyway. One section entered because another had given it something to answer.

I thought of Mother driving to South Union each week, carrying things she had never learned to say aloud.

The week I had given to band camp had cost us the October trip. It was not an enormous loss.

That did not make it meaningless.

I had spent years believing sacrifice announced itself through the size of what was surrendered. But band rarely asked families for one enormous thing. It asked for hundreds of smaller ones, each reasonable when considered alone.

A Saturday.

A tank of gas.

A repaired wheel.

A meal labeled correctly.

A trip postponed.

Six gallons of tea.

The audience would never see most of those offerings. From the stands, they would see walls move, hear music, and perhaps feel something they could not name. They would not see the parent who left work early, the father who repaired the porch, the mother who checked every ingredient, or the grandmother who stood behind a drink table making sure every child had something cold.

Simple gifts.

Small enough to escape notice.

Large enough to make the show possible.

Molly stopped humming.

“Mom?”

“Yes?”

“Do you think the show is good?”

I looked through the windshield at the road shining ahead of us.

Parts of the show were beautiful. Parts were confusing. Some walls moved because the music required them to move. Others still crossed the field because someone had written a count on a drill sheet.

The band sounded young. Promising. Small in certain moments and larger in others.

“I think it’s becoming good.”

Molly turned toward me.

“That sounds like something Mr. Mercer would say.”

“I’m sorry.”

“He says we’re not allowed to decide what the show is before we finish making it.”

“That sounds reasonable.”

Which was probably why it annoyed her.

Molly watched the houses pass behind the rain-streaked window. She traced one drop with her finger until it joined another and disappeared beneath the edge of the glass.

“Sam thinks the house is too obvious,” she said.

“What do you think?”

“I think he complains about everything.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

She gave me the same look I had given her hundreds of times—eyebrows lifted, lips pressed together, patience reduced to a courtesy.

Apparently, motherhood could be inherited through facial expression.

“I think he may be right about some of it,” she admitted.

“Which parts?”

“The walls. He says if we have to keep showing people it’s a house, maybe the music isn’t doing enough.”

That was remarkably perceptive for a fourteen-year-old boy who had recently asked how anyone could live beneath the Kentucky sky without thinking about its size.

“What did you tell him?”

“That playing saxophone doesn’t make him the show designer.”

“And he said playing clarinet doesn’t make you one either.”

Molly turned toward me so quickly that the seat belt caught against her shoulder.

“How did you know?”

“I’ve met both of you.”

Her mouth tightened.

“He has a point,” I said.

“You’re supposed to be on my side.”

“I am.”

“It doesn’t sound like it.”

“Being on your side does not require me to believe you are always right.”

She settled back against the seat and returned her attention to the window.

“Grandma thinks it does.”

That was true enough that I had no response.

As we reached the edge of town, the western sky widened ahead of us. The last heavy clouds were moving away, their dark undersides edged by the pale light beyond them.

Somewhere to the west, South Union stood on land where people had once tried to build a life around shared work, careful hands, useful objects, and music simple enough to be remembered.

I wondered whether they had known which ordinary days would survive them.

Which table.

Which chair.

Which melody.

Probably not.

Life rarely tells us what will remain. It does not identify the moment that will be remembered or warn us when an ordinary kindness is about to outlive the person who offered it.

It simply asks us to make something while we are here.

Beside me, Molly began humming again.

The melody emerged so softly that, at first, I heard only the movement of her breath. Then it found the same phrase she had played in the hallway.

This time, I joined her.

Not loudly.

Not particularly well.

Molly turned toward me.

I expected her to stop. At fourteen, she had begun protecting certain parts of herself from witnesses, particularly witnesses who had given birth to her.

Instead, she smiled.

It was not a large smile. Just a small lifting at one corner of her mouth before she returned to the beginning and slowed the melody enough for me to find it.

For the next half mile, we sang without words.

The tune was small enough to fit inside the car.

It was also large enough to carry us home.
